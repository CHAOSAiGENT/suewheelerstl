# Go-to-Market Overhaul — Meeting Summary
## Wood Refinishing by Sue Wheeler
**Date:** Transcript 06 | **Duration:** ~2 hours | **Participants:** Peter Wheeler, Sue Wheeler

---

## Executive Summary

This was the definitive product/market strategy session. Peter and Sue walked the entire business through a whiteboard exercise to answer three questions: **What do you offer? What do people actually call you about? What work do you want to be doing?** The result is a tiered service map, a geographic focus, a naming/terminology cleanup, and a clear prioritization framework that directly feeds website content, marketing messaging, and the bidding system.

The core outcome: **Stop talking about processes. Start talking about results.** Strip and refinish is *how* Sue works. It's not *why* customers call. Customers call because their front door looks terrible, their staircase doesn't match their new floors, or a burst pipe destroyed their woodwork. The entire go-to-market shifts from process-first language to outcome-first language.

## 1. SERVICE ARCHITECTURE — Final Structure

### Two Master Categories

**Architecture** (the core business — where all growth investment goes)
- Strip & Refinish (S&R) — process, not a top-level message
- Perk Up & Protect (PUP) — standalone service, aspirational priority
- Finished Carpentry — aspirational, Brian-dependent
- Insurance Bids — billable service category

**Furniture** (deprioritized — heading toward elimination)
- Refinish only (includes strip, color, repair, finish)
- No touch-ups, no PUP on furniture
- No repair-only furniture work
- Refer out for repair-only requests (Custom Furniture Works, Richie's) — but route through Sue, not direct

### Terminology Lockdown

| Term | Means | Does NOT Mean |
|------|-------|---------------|
| **Woodwork** | Stripping and refinishing of in-place architectural woodwork (door frames, window frames, baseboards, etc.) | General carpentry or broad catch-all |
| **Carpentry** | Finished carpentry — wainscoting, beadboard, chair rail, baseboards, storm doors. NOT framing | Rough carpentry, structural work |
| **Strip & Refinish** | The *process* used to achieve results. Chemical strip, sand, stain, polyurethane | A customer-facing product name — customers don't search for this |
| **Perk Up & Protect** | Standalone maintenance service: wet sand, color, topcoat. Architecture only | Furniture touch-ups (those don't exist) |
| **Color Matching** | A *result/benefit* of the work, not a standalone service. Applies across PUP, S&R, and carpentry | A product line or category |
| **Strip Only** | First half of S&R, offered as standalone. Customer gets a clean baseline for DIY | A preferred offering — Sue prefers doing both halves |

## 3. THE MESSAGING SHIFT — Process → Results

**The fundamental reframe of the entire business's external communication:**

### Old Way (How Sue Has Always Talked)
- "We strip and refinish"
- "We hand strip, never dip"
- "We do EPA certified lead paint removal"
- Process, process, process

### New Way (What Customers Actually Care About)
- "Your front door looks new again"
- "Your staircase matches your new floors"
- "The water damage is gone"
- "No harsh chemicals needed" (PUP)
- "We added wainscoting that looks like it's been there 100 years"
- Results, outcomes, problems solved

### The Rule
**Strip & Refinish is never the headline. It's always the answer to "how do you do it?"**

The conversation flow should be:
1. Customer has a problem (water damage, ugly door, mismatched colors, painted-over woodwork)
2. Sue determines the solution (PUP, S&R, Finished Carpentry)
3. The process is explained as *the method*, not the product

This changes everything — website copy, social posts, bid language, how Sue describes the business on the phone.

## 5. THE "OFF MENU" RULE — Business Discipline

Peter established a strict operating rule for how Sue handles work requests:

### The Restaurant Analogy
Sue's business has been a Cheesecake Factory — a sprawling menu where "sure, we can do that" adds items nobody planned for. The goal is a **bistro menu** — tight, profitable, every item optimized.

### The Rules

**Rule 1: No Daily Specials**
If it's not on the menu, it doesn't get ordered. Period. Even if Sue has the skills and materials. Even if it's easy.

**Rule 2: Enhancement vs. "Sure We Can Do That"**
Finished carpentry work is SOLD as an enhancement to existing jobs (e.g., "While we're doing your front door, let's also redo that porch ceiling"). It's never "oh yeah, we can do that too while we're here."

If a customer asks for something:
- **If it's on the finished carpentry list** → Sell it as an enhancement. It's a real line item.
- **If it's NOT on the list** → Either add it to the list and start selling it properly, or decline.
- **Never** say "sure, we can do that" for unplanned work

**Rule 3: Free Desserts Are Fine — New Menu Items Are Not**
Sue can give away anything that's already on the menu (a freebie repair for a loyal customer, absorbing a small fix). She cannot create a new item for free or at cost. The banister trim for the whole-house customer? That's a repair — it's on the menu, and she's gifting it. That's fine. Building something that's never been offered before? Not fine without adding it to the menu first.

**Rule 4: Progressive Menu Reduction**
As Tier 0 items mature and Tier 1 items dominate revenue, lower tiers get cut. The end state could be: PUP + Finished Carpentry only, with everything else eliminated. Furniture is already heading there. Lead paint removal could follow. Insurance bids could follow. The goal is a business that could be sold — predictable process, predictable pricing, consistent growth, tight menu.

## 6. WEBSITE IMPLICATIONS — Direct Actions

### Homepage Above the Fold
- Lead with outcomes, not processes
- Both service tiers visible: S&R (the guaranteed solution) and PUP (the faster/cheaper/chemical-free alternative)
- Sue determines which one — the customer doesn't pick
- Contact mechanism immediately accessible

### Service Pages Need
- **Doors** — dedicated page, best portfolio, most confidence
- **Staircases** — dedicated page
- **Kitchens** — dedicated page (butler's pantry lives here)
- **Architectural Woodwork** — dedicated page, the niche/specialist message
- **Perk Up & Protect** — dedicated page, educational (creating a category)
- **Finished Carpentry** — mention/soft-launch page, not hard sell yet
- **Furniture** — minimal presence, "we specialize in architectural woodwork" language steers away

### What NOT to Put on the Site
- Published pricing (no more "$X per staircase" on the website)
- "Neighborhood stripper" or inside-joke branding — means nothing to customers
- Process-first language as headlines (hand strip never dip is a sub-point, not a headline)
- Lead paint removal as a featured service
- Anything that positions Sue as a generalist

### What TO Put on the Site
- Neighborhood-specific content referencing Tier A/B/C areas
- Before/after portfolio (after photos alone are fine if that's what exists — tell the story)
- Insurance bid information (it's a real service with real pricing)
- Historic home expertise as a differentiator
- EPA certification as a trust badge, not a service headline
- Color as a benefit woven throughout, not a standalone offering

## 7. BEFORE & AFTER PHOTOS — Adjusted Expectations

Sue acknowledged she forgets to take photos. Peter's reframe:

- **After-only photos are fine.** Tell the story with words, show the result with the image.
- **Before photos are really only compelling for catastrophe scenarios** (which is most of Sue's work anyway)
- **Prioritize photos of Tier 0 and Tier 1 work** — doors, stairs, kitchens, architectural woodwork, PUP results, finished carpentry
- The beadboard ceiling job, the partial staircase strip, the dog-damaged door — these are the stories worth capturing
- **Partial work** (like the half-stripped staircase that went viral on Facebook) has engagement value even if it's "off menu" work Sue is phasing out

## 8. KEY BUSINESS INSIGHTS FROM THE SESSION

### Sue's "Ah-Ha" Moments
- PUP is architectural only — never furniture. This was definitively settled.
- Color matching is a result, not a product. Stop treating it as a service line.
- Finished Carpentry is an *enhancement* to existing jobs, not a standalone business.
- The Cheesecake Factory → Bistro analogy clicked. "Cut it."
- "This is a business plan." — Sue recognized this session went way beyond a website review.

### Peter's Strategic Observations
- Sue has been selling how she works, not what she accomplishes. The entire external voice needs to flip.
- The bias toward doors being #1 is real AND correct — process, price, and panache all check out.
- Furniture is a legacy anchor. Every job taken is a job that could've been a door, staircase, or kitchen.
- The "sure we can do that" habit is the #1 revenue leak. It creates unpredictable scope, unpredictable pricing, and schedule disruption.
- If the end game is selling the business, the menu has to be tight. "Nobody wants to buy a Cheesecake Factory."

### Unresolved Items
- Architectural woodwork as Tier 1 vs. Tier 2 — Peter thinks it's too broad a category for Tier 1 messaging; Sue insists it's her niche. **Settled: It stays Tier 1 because Sue wants to own that space in her target neighborhoods.**
- Lafayette Square complexity — moved from Tier A to Tier B neighborhoods. Worth understanding why (access? parking? customer type?).
- Brian's single-point-of-failure status on finished carpentry. No contingency plan discussed.
