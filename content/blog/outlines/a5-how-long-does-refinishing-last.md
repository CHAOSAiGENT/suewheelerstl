## Meta Description
Properly refinished wood with polyurethane can last 10–20 years before it needs attention again. The real answer depends on the finish used, the surface type, and how it was applied. Here's what actually determines longevity.

### H2: The Short Answer — And Why It Varies (~120 words)
- Properly applied oil-based polyurethane on a hand-stripped, correctly prepped surface: 10–20 years on low-to-medium traffic surfaces
- High-traffic surfaces (stair treads, bar tops, kitchen cabinet doors): expect 7–10 years before noticeable wear warrants attention
- What "attention" means: not necessarily a full strip-and-redo — often a maintenance coat (light sand + fresh topcoat) extends life significantly
- Variables that affect longevity:
  - Finish type (oil-based polyurethane vs. water-based vs. lacquer vs. wax)
  - Surface traffic level (stair tread vs. built-in bookcase shelf)
  - Application quality (number of coats, sanding between coats, proper cure time)
  - Prep quality (how well the old finish was removed before new finish was applied)
  - Ongoing maintenance (cleaning, UV protection on exterior pieces)


### H2: Finish Type Is the Biggest Factor (~150 words)
- Not all finishes are created equal — and many refinishers use whatever's cheapest or fastest to apply
- Polyurethane (oil-based): the gold standard for architectural wood; hard, durable, amber tone; bonds well to wood; appropriate cure time is 24–48 hours per coat; 3–4 coats typical
- Polyurethane (water-based): faster dry time, lower odor, clearer tone (less amber); slightly less hard than oil-based; good for lighter wood species or contemporary aesthetics
- What to avoid: lacquer (brittle, cracks under movement), shellac (not waterproof), wax (requires constant reapplication, not protective for heavy use), tung oil (absorbs, doesn't build a protective film, needs frequent reapplication)
- Sue's position: polyurethane only — oil or water-based depending on wood species, project context, and client goal. Never marine varnish, lacquer, shellac, or tung oil on architectural wood.
- Why it matters: a piece finished with wax or lacquer may look good at first and fail within 2–3 years; a piece finished with oil-based poly will still look good a decade later

### H2: Surface and Traffic Level (~120 words)
- Not all surfaces wear the same — group by expected longevity:
- Long life (15–20+ years with proper care):
  - Built-in bookcases, hutches, buffets
  - Room millwork (baseboards, crown molding, chair rail)
  - Mantels
  - Interior doors (low-contact surfaces)
- Medium life (10–15 years):
  - Kitchen cabinet doors and frames (daily contact, cleaning products)
  - Stair railings and newel posts (hand contact)
  - Interior door edges and jambs (friction wear)
- Higher wear (7–10 years before attention):
  - Stair treads (direct foot traffic)
  - Bar tops and restaurant tabletops (heavy daily use, cleaning products, spills)
  - Front doors (UV exposure, moisture, seasonal expansion/contraction)
- Note: "attention" doesn't always mean full refinishing — a maintenance coat (sand + fresh topcoat) on a well-prepped surface extends life significantly at lower cost than starting over


### H2: Application Quality — Why It's Not Just About the Product (~120 words)
- Two identical cans of polyurethane can produce very different results depending on application
- Factors that affect finish life:
  - Surface prep: if the old finish wasn't fully removed, or the wood wasn't sanded correctly, the new finish won't bond properly — it peels sooner
  - Number of coats: 2 coats may look fine at first; 3–4 coats with sanding between each is what actually holds up
  - Sanding between coats: creates mechanical adhesion; skipping it produces a finish that may look smooth but delaminate with time
  - Cure time: polyurethane needs 24–48 hours per coat to cure properly; rushing the process produces a soft finish that wears faster
- The shortcut always shows up later — usually within 2–5 years when the finish starts lifting or wearing unevenly

### H2: What Maintenance Extends Life (~100 words)
- Simple habits that add years:
  - Clean with a damp cloth — avoid saturating wood with water
  - Use a wood-safe cleaner (no ammonia, no bleach-based products)
  - For exterior doors: UV-protective treatment on the finish if the door is south or west facing
  - Avoid rubber-backed mats on refinished floors (traps moisture)
  - Report small chips or scratches early — a tiny touchup is much less expensive than a full refinishing
- Maintenance coat option: if the finish is worn but the wood beneath is sound, a light sand and fresh topcoat (no full strip) can restore protection and appearance — significantly less expensive than starting over
