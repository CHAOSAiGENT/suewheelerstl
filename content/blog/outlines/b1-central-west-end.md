## Meta Description
Central West End homes contain some of St. Louis's finest original woodwork — pocket doors, built-in hutches, ornate staircases. Here's what we see most in CWE, and why it's worth preserving.

## Outline

### Introduction (~80 words)
- CWE is one of St. Louis's great historic neighborhoods — pre-1900 architecture, tree-lined streets, homes built when woodwork was art
- The wood in these homes is not decorative trim from a big-box store; it's original, site-built millwork from a century ago
- Problem: neglect, repaints over original grain, decades of accumulated finish
- Opportunity: these pieces can come back — and refinishing them costs a fraction of what replacement would
- Internal link: [Neighborhoods — Central West End](/neighborhoods/central-west-end/)

### H2: The Woodwork You Find in Central West End (~150 words)
- Describe the dominant architectural styles in CWE (Queen Anne, Romanesque Revival, Colonial Revival, early 20th-century)
- Common wood features found in CWE homes:
  - Pocket doors: sliding panel doors recessed into walls — often oak or chestnut, original hardware; common in formal entry and parlor transitions
  - Built-in hutches and buffets: dining room built-ins with leaded glass and original hardware; often oak or walnut
  - Staircases: grand entry staircases with turned spindles, ornate newel posts, carved railings; high visual impact
  - Original interior doors: paneled doors with original hardware — all woodwork from the same millwork run
  - Wainscoting and chair rail: formal rooms with full wood paneling programs
  - Mantels: original fireplace surrounds, often with carved detail and period-specific profiles
- Note: the wood in CWE homes is often white oak, red oak, chestnut, or walnut — species that refinish beautifully when properly stripped and finished

### H2: What We're Usually Called to Fix (~120 words)
- The most common conditions we encounter in CWE homes:
  - Painted-over original wood: previous owners painted oak, chestnut, or walnut in an attempt to "modernize" — often recoverable
  - Accumulated finish buildup: 5–7 layers of finish over 100 years creates a thick, yellowed, uneven coating that requires full strip
  - Worn stair treads: heavy foot traffic on original oak treads; finish worn through in walking paths
  - Swollen or stuck pocket doors: old finish and humidity warping that prevents smooth operation
  - Water damage to hutch shelves: minor moisture penetration causing clouding or discoloration in finish
  - Missing or mismatched hardware: often found on built-ins and pocket doors — we work around the hardware, not against it
- Most of these are fixable without replacing the wood — the piece just needs proper attention

### H2: EPA Certification and Pre-1978 Homes in CWE (~100 words)
- Virtually all CWE homes were built before 1978 — which means any refinishing work on original wood may involve lead paint
- What this means: federal law (EPA RRP Rule) requires that any contractor disturbing painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes be EPA Certified in Lead Renovation, Repair & Painting
- Sue Wheeler holds this certification — it's not an add-on; it's part of every pre-1978 job
- What lead-safe protocol covers: containment, proper stripping technique, disposal, post-work cleaning and testing
- Internal link: [Historic Tax Credits](/historic-preservation/historic-tax-credits/) — CWE is in a qualifying historic district; refinishing costs may be eligible
- Internal link: [Secretary of Interior's Standards](/historic-preservation/secretary-of-interior-standards/) — hand-strip compliance for tax-credit-eligible projects

### H2: Refinish or Replace? The CWE Math (~100 words)
- For original woodwork in a CWE home, replacement almost never makes sense — economically or architecturally
- Why replacement fails:
  - Custom millwork profiles from the 1890s–1920s cannot be reproduced cheaply; custom mill runs start at hundreds per linear foot
  - New wood doesn't match the character, density, or aging of old-growth lumber
  - Replacement requires permits and disruption; refinishing in most cases does not
- The math: a full staircase refinishing typically runs a fraction of what staircase replacement would cost
- The character argument: a refinished original staircase looks better and adds more to home value than a replacement that doesn't match the house
