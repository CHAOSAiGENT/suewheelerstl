## Outline

### Intro (75 words)
- Open with a common institutional scenario: a facilities manager gets quotes for refinishing the church doors or the university lobby paneling, treats it like a painting job, hires a generalist
- The work looks fine for six months. Then the finish starts lifting. Or the lead-safe documentation is inadequate for compliance review. Or the refinisher didn't understand that the building had occupancy constraints.
- Thesis: institutional wood refinishing is a distinct discipline — and the gaps between a generalist and a specialist show up in real ways

---

### H2: What Makes Institutional Wood Different (200 words)
- **Age and origin:** Most institutional buildings with original woodwork were built before 1940. That means pre-WWII construction methods, original-growth timber (denser, different porosity), and layers of finish history that a residential refinisher may not know how to read.
- **Scale:** A single set of institutional entry doors may weigh 200+ pounds per leaf, with large flat panels that reveal every imperfection in a finish. Scale changes the process.
- **Complexity of existing finish:** Institutional wood is often coated with decades of paint, varnish, oil, or wax — sometimes in incompatible layers. Diagnosis before stripping is essential.
- **Detail:** Carved ornament, applied moldings, leaded glass adjacency — institutional millwork requires care that a production-oriented refinisher won't provide
- **Public exposure:** This is wood that thousands of people interact with. A finish failure is visible in a way that a residential failure is not.

---

### H2: The Compliance Layer Residential Refinishers Aren't Prepared For (200 words)
- **EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule (RRP):** Any pre-1978 building with lead paint requires an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. This is mandatory — not optional — and documentation requirements for institutional buildings (especially those receiving federal funds or in compliance programs) are significant.
- **Secretary of Interior's Standards:** Historic venues, churches on the National Register, university buildings in certified historic districts — all may require Standards-compliant work. That means the method, not just the outcome, is subject to review.
- **Insurance and bonding:** Institutional clients often require contractor insurance levels that residential-only refinishers don't carry
- **Documentation:** Scope of work, products used, MSDS sheets, lead certification copies — institutional clients frequently need this for their own records or grant compliance. A generalist may not even know what to provide.

---

### H2: Scheduling and Coordination in an Active Institution (150 words)
- Churches have liturgical calendars. Concert halls have event seasons. Universities have academic schedules. None of them close for a week of refinishing.
- Work must be phased around the institution's schedule — sometimes at night, sometimes on Sundays, sometimes in sections that allow partial use
- Coordinating with other trades on a renovation project adds another layer: wood refinishing often needs to happen before certain installations and after certain demolition — sequencing discipline matters
- Fume management: in a shared public building, the refinishing schedule has to account for ventilation and cure time before re-occupancy

---

### H2: What Questions to Ask an Institutional Refinisher (150 words)
Checklist framing — what a facilities manager or architect should ask before hiring:
- Are you EPA Certified Lead Renovator? Can you provide documentation?
- Have you worked in buildings subject to the Secretary of Interior's Standards? 
- Can you provide a work plan and product list for our records?
- How do you handle scheduling around an active building?
- What institutional projects have you completed in St. Louis? (Ask for references)
- How do you assess existing finish layers before deciding on a stripping method?
