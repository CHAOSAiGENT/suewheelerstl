## SECTION: The Central West End and its woodwork

Westminster Place. Pershing Avenue. Lindell Boulevard. Maryland Avenue. The Central West End was developed at the peak of St. Louis's Gilded Age expansion, and its builders spent money on the interiors. Old-growth white oak, quarter-sawn for stability. Douglas fir with a grain so tight it takes stain like glass. Chestnut before the blight took it.

These aren't materials you can buy today. What's in a CWE home was milled from trees that were old when the house was built, and it will outlast any replacement if it's properly maintained. What it can't survive is being buried under sixty years of paint, wax, and neglect.

The pocket doors in these homes — and nearly every CWE row house has them — slide between the walls on original hardware, still functional after more than a century. The staircase halls are architectural statements: elaborate newel posts, turned spindles, continuous curved railings that rise two or three flights. The butler's pantries have built-in cabinetry that was designed as part of the house. These details are why people buy these homes.

## SECTION: What we refinish in the Central West End

CWE projects tend to be comprehensive. Rarely does a homeowner call about just one door. More often it's a staircase plus the pocket doors on two floors plus the dining room built-ins — all of it gone dull or painted over at once.

**Pocket doors.** CWE homes were built with pocket doors as a standard feature, not a luxury option. Most are original fir or oak — solid, tight-grained, still sound. We remove each door from its pocket, hand-strip every face and edge in the shop, refinish to match the surrounding millwork, and reinstall. The hardware stays intact and working.

**Staircases.** The entry staircases in Central West End homes are the first thing you see when you walk in. Carved newel posts. Turned spindles. Continuous railings that follow the curve of the stair. All of it stripped and refinished by hand — dipping would destroy the glue joints and raise grain across every tread. We do it the way it was done originally.

**Architectural millwork.** Door and window surrounds, baseboards, crown molding, picture rails, wainscoting — the full millwork package in CWE homes is typically consistent across the public rooms, which means it all needs to match. We work systematically so the result looks like the house, not like piecemeal repairs.

**Kitchen cabinets and butler's pantries.** Original butler's pantry cabinetry in CWE homes is frequently in better shape than the kitchen itself. Hand-built face frames, solid wood doors, glass-front upper cabinets. We restore them rather than replace them — the quality of the original is almost always superior to anything new.

## SECTION: Restoring original woodwork vs. replacing it

Replacement sounds simple until you try to match what's already there. Old-growth fir and quarter-sawn oak are not available new at residential scale. The profiles on original CWE millwork — door surrounds, baseboards, crown — were milled on-site or by local millworks that no longer exist. Getting close costs money and time. Getting it right is nearly impossible.

Restoration is the better answer in almost every case. The wood is dense and stable. The profiles are intact. Under layers of failed finish, the original surface is typically in good condition — just buried. A skilled refinisher strips it without damaging the wood, repairs any wear or damage, and applies a finish that lets the original material show.

The result is woodwork that looks the way it was intended to look — and that no amount of new material can replicate.

## SECTION: Lead paint and pre-1978 homes in the Central West End

Every home in the Central West End predates 1978. Most predate 1940. Lead paint is present on virtually all original woodwork — sometimes under one layer of latex, sometimes under a dozen.

The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rule requires that anyone disturbing lead paint in a pre-1978 home be a Certified Lead Renovator. Sue Wheeler is EPA Certified. Every project includes proper containment, HEPA vacuuming, and documentation — protecting your household and keeping your project compliant with federal law.

If you've had other contractors work on woodwork in your home without mentioning lead, ask about their certification. It matters for your family's safety and for the validity of the work.

## SECTION: FAQ

**Q: How long does it take to refinish a staircase in a CWE home?**
A staircase scope — treads, risers, spindles, newel post, and railing — typically runs one to two weeks depending on size and condition. We work in sections so the staircase stays passable. We'll walk you through the schedule before we start.

**Q: Can you match the finish on my existing floors?**
Yes. Stain matching to existing floors or millwork is standard practice. We bring samples on the estimate visit and test on a hidden area before committing to the full finish.

**Q: My pocket doors have been painted shut for years. Can they be repaired?**
In most cases, yes. We remove the doors from the pocket, assess the hardware and frame condition, and make repairs as needed. Original pocket door hardware is typically reusable and often better quality than modern replacements.

**Q: Do you work on the exterior of front doors in the CWE?**
Yes. Front door refinishing — strip, refinish, and rehang — is one of our most common scopes in the neighborhood. Sun and weather take a toll on south and west-facing doors especially. We do this in our shop for consistent results, then rehang on-site.

**Q: How do I know if refinishing makes sense versus replacement?**
If the wood is sound — no rot, no structural damage — refinishing almost always makes sense. We'll tell you honestly at the estimate if we see anything that changes that calculus. We don't push projects that aren't worth doing.
