## BODY

### H2: Your original wood cabinets are worth more than their current finish suggests.

Stained kitchen cabinets in St. Louis's older homes are almost always solid wood or solid-wood-faced — oak from the 1970s and 80s, cherry and walnut in pre-war homes, maple and birch in mid-century builds. The grain in these materials developed over decades. It's tight, dense, and visually distinctive in a way that modern cabinet lumber can't replicate.

The cabinets themselves are usually fine. It's the finish that's failing. Faded color, water rings near the sink, grease buildup around the handles, worn edges where doors are grabbed daily — these are surface problems. The wood underneath is sound. Refinishing treats the problem without touching what isn't broken.

New kitchen cabinets in a typical St. Louis home cost $20,000–$50,000 or more, depending on scope and materials. Refinishing the originals costs a fraction of that and produces a kitchen that reads as completely updated to anyone who walks in.

### H2: Hand-strip method — why it matters for stained cabinets.

Dip stripping destroys wood grain. Submerging cabinets in a caustic chemical tank raises the grain fibers, opens the pores, and permanently changes the surface texture. The stain that goes on afterward looks blotchy and uneven because the wood itself has been damaged.

Hand stripping preserves the grain. We remove the old finish layer by layer with appropriate solvents and detail tools, keeping the surface intact. The new stain penetrates cleanly and evenly. The result looks like the wood it actually is — not like a surface that's been processed.

This matters more for stained cabinets than for painted ones, because the grain is visible in the finished result. Damaged grain shows.

### H2: Stain matching — to itself, or to the rest of your woodwork.

The most common request on stained cabinet projects: match the refinished cabinets to the existing hardwood floors, door trim, or other millwork in the room. This is a skill that takes years to develop, and it's one of the things that separates a refinishing specialist from a general contractor.

We assess the existing tones — accounting for the fact that original finishes shift in color over decades due to UV exposure and oxidation — and work to get as close as technically possible. When an exact match isn't achievable, we discuss options before we start. You don't get surprised at the end.

We can also update the stain color entirely — most commonly, taking dated honey-oak from the 1980s and 90s to a more contemporary tone. The grain still shows; the color reads completely differently. → [Learn more about cabinet color change](/kitchen-cabinet-refinishing/cabinet-color-change/)

### H2: Strip & Refinish or Perk Up & Protect?

**Strip & Refinish:** Full restoration. Strip to bare wood, stain, polyurethane finish. For cabinets with failing or worn finish, water damage, color change requests, or any surface damage. This is the right choice for most stained cabinets that haven't been properly addressed in years.

**Perk Up & Protect:** Light prep and fresh topcoat over a solid existing finish. For cabinets that are holding their finish structurally but looking dull and tired. We'll tell you honestly which your cabinets need when we see them.

### H2: EPA Certified for pre-1978 kitchens.

If your home was built before 1978, the finish on your cabinets may contain lead. Stripping those cabinets generates lead dust — in your kitchen. Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every pre-1978 project is handled with proper containment, HEPA filtration, and documented cleanup.

**Q: Can you keep the same stain color, or does refinishing change it?**
A: We can match the existing stain color as closely as possible. The new finish will look like the original cabinets did when they were first finished — which may be lighter or richer than what's currently on them, since the old finish has faded and shifted. If you want to keep the existing look, we work to replicate it. If you want to update it, we discuss options.

**Q: How long do refinished stained cabinets last?**
A: With proper hand-strip prep and a quality polyurethane topcoat, 10–15 years before the finish needs attention is a reasonable expectation with normal use. A Perk Up & Protect maintenance coat partway through can extend that further. The quality of the prep determines the longevity of the finish — this is why skipping the strip is a mistake.

**Q: Do you refinish just the door faces and drawer fronts, or the whole cabinet?**
A: We address the full cabinet system — door faces, drawer fronts, and the frames and boxes where they're visible. Door faces and drawer fronts get the most wear and are the first priority, but refinishing them against unaddressed frames produces an inconsistent result. We scope what makes sense for your kitchen at the estimate stage.

**Q: How long will my kitchen be out of commission?**
A: For an average kitchen, plan on 5–10 business days from strip to final cure. During active work the kitchen is inaccessible; we stage the work to minimize total disruption. We discuss timing and sequencing at the estimate so you can plan around it.

**Q: My oak cabinets have that orange honey tone from the 1990s. Can you fix that?**
A: Yes. That orange-honey oak finish is one of the most common refinishing requests we get. We strip it, neutralize the grain, and apply a more contemporary stain.
