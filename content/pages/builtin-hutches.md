## BODY

### H2: These pieces were built into your home. There is no modern equivalent.

The built-in hutches and buffets in St. Louis's Craftsman, Victorian, and Colonial homes were constructed as part of the original architectural specification — designed for the room, built from old-growth lumber, fitted by hand. White oak, quarter-sawn oak, chestnut, heart pine: the materials in these pieces have density and character that modern lumber cannot replicate.

The profiles were milled specifically for the project. The proportions were worked out to the room. A built-in hutch in a 1905 Compton Heights dining room wasn't ordered from a catalog — it was made for that house.

New cabinetry built to approximate the original is a compromise: different wood species, different grain density, machine-cut profiles that read as reproduction rather than original. The original piece is always better than what replaces it. Refinishing is the approach that keeps it.

**We work on built-in pieces only — not freestanding furniture.** If a hutch, buffet, or bookcase is built into the wall structure or is a fixed architectural feature of the room, it's in our scope. Freestanding furniture is not.

### H2: What built-in hutch refinishing involves.

Built-in pieces are worked on in place — they're too large to remove safely, and in most cases are built into the wall structure. We use appropriate solvents and hand tools to strip the existing finish, working with detail tools through any molding profiles, recessed panels, or carved elements.

After stripping, we assess the wood condition. Minor surface damage — checks, worn edges, small cracks — is typically addressable as part of the Strip & Refinish scope. Then stain to match or update the existing tone, and polyurethane topcoat.

For pieces with glass inserts — hutch doors with original divided lights, leaded glass — we mask carefully around the glass. We do not remove original glass for refinishing. The risk of cracking irreplaceable glass in handling isn't worth it.

### H2: The dining room is where the finish condition gets noticed.

Built-in hutches and buffets live in dining rooms — formal spaces, the rooms where guests spend time, the rooms that appear first in listing photos. A dining room hutch with failing finish, painted-over wood, or a dated color is impossible to ignore.

A refinished dining room hutch transforms the entire room. The woodwork that looked dark and heavy becomes the feature it was always designed to be. The original proportions and materials read correctly when the finish is right.

### H2: Perk Up & Protect — for pieces that don't need a full strip.

If your built-in is in good structural condition and still holding its finish but looking dull and tired, a **Perk Up & Protect** maintenance coat may be the right approach. Light preparation and a fresh topcoat restore the appearance without the full Strip & Refinish scope and timeline. We'll tell you which your piece needs when we see it.

### H2: Stewardship — the right framing.

The people who built these homes invested in quality materials and skilled craftsmen because they expected the work to outlast them. Every homeowner since has been the steward of that investment. Refinishing rather than replacing is the act that honors it — and the one that keeps the authentic material in the room.

### H2: EPA Certified for pre-1978 built-ins.

The finish on built-in cabinetry in homes built before 1978 almost certainly contains lead. In a dining room where food is present, this matters. Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every pre-1978 project includes proper containment, HEPA filtration, and documented cleanup.

**Q: What counts as a built-in vs. freestanding furniture?**
A: A built-in is a piece that is structurally integrated into the room — attached to walls, framed into the room structure, or part of the original architectural specification of the home. A dining room hutch that's bolted to the wall and has no back-of-cabinet visible is a built-in. A freestanding china cabinet that can be moved is furniture. We work on built-in architectural pieces only. If you're not sure, send us a photo when you call and we'll tell you.

**Q: Can you strip a painted hutch back to the original wood?**
A: Usually yes. Painted-over original wood is one of the most common conditions we see on built-in hutches in St. Louis's historic homes — they were painted over in a mid-century renovation and never stripped back. In most cases the original wood surface is intact underneath. We strip carefully and assess before committing to a result. When the original wood is recoverable, the transformation is often dramatic.

**Q: Do you repair built-ins as part of the refinishing project?**
A: Minor repairs are often addressable within a refinish scope — small cracks, worn edges, loose molding, surface checks. Structural damage (severely warped panels, failed joinery, missing sections of carved detail) is assessed individually. We tell you honestly what's within refinishing scope and what requires a separate carpentry engagement before we start.

**Q: My hutch has leaded glass inserts. Can you refinish around them?**
A: Yes. Masking around glass inserts — including divided lights and leaded glass — is standard procedure. We do not remove original glass to refinish the surrounding wood. The risk of cracking irreplaceable glass in handling isn't worth taking, and careful masking produces a clean result without removal.
