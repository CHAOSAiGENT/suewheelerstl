## SECTION: These neighborhoods. This woodwork.

**H2:** Central West End. Benton Park. Compton Heights. Lafayette Square. Shaw. The homes that define St. Louis.

The homes in St. Louis's historic neighborhoods were built between roughly 1880 and 1940. Many of them are still standing with their original woodwork intact. Original oak door frames. Original heart pine baseboards. Wainscoting that was custom-installed for the room. Built-in hutches that were designed specifically for the dining room they occupy. Front doors with original profiles and glass panels.

This woodwork is irreplaceable in the most literal sense. The wood species aren't available commercially. The profile geometries were made with custom millwork equipment that no longer exists. The construction methods — mortise-and-tenon joinery, hand-fitted profiles, hand-rubbed finishes — aren't part of how woodwork gets made today.

When original woodwork in a historic home is damaged or removed, it can be approximated with modern materials. But it can't be replaced. The new piece will look close, but not right. Anyone who knows what they're looking at will notice.

Refinishing is the only path that keeps what's actually there. That's what this work is.

## SECTION: What comes up in historic home projects.

**H2:** The specific restoration work these homes typically need.

Historic homes in St. Louis are largely intact as architecture. What they usually need is not structural work — it's surface work. The original finishes are 40, 60, or 100 years old. They've been painted over, layered, ignored, or badly maintained. Getting them back requires knowing what's underneath and how to remove what's on top without damaging the original material.

**The most common situations we see:**

**Painted-over stained woodwork.** Original oak or walnut woodwork was stained and varnished, then painted over at some point in the 1960s or 1970s when that was considered an update. Stripping that paint carefully — by hand, not with dip tanks — recovers the original grain and profile. The wood underneath is almost always extraordinary.

**Original doors with failed finish.** Pre-1920s interior doors often have finish that was applied once in 1910 and never properly maintained. The shellac or early varnish has gone opaque, cracked, or partially lifted. Hand-stripping back to bare wood and applying a modern polyurethane finish restores function and appearance for another several decades.

**Millwork with heavy paint buildup.** Multiple layers of paint on crown molding, wainscoting, and door casings obscure the original profiles. Hand-stripping carefully reveals geometry that's still intact underneath. In most cases the profiles are sharper and more interesting than anything being made today.

**Original staircases.** Treads, risers, newel posts, handrails, and balusters in pre-1940s homes were made from hard species — white oak, yellow pine, old-growth fir — that refinish to a level of beauty that new stair parts can't match. Original staircase components are worth restoring even when they appear beyond saving.

## SECTION: Why hand-stripping matters for historic homes.

**H2:** The original material only survives if the stripping method is right.

The distinction between hand-stripping and dip-tank stripping matters in every refinishing project. For historic homes, it's decisive.

Dip-tank stripping — where pieces are submerged in a hot chemical bath — is fast and inexpensive. It's also destructive to historic material. The heat and chemicals raise the grain, swell profiles, attack glue joints, and drive residue into the pores of the wood. On a pre-1920s door with original mortise-and-tenon joinery and hand-applied profiles, dipping is the wrong method. Pieces come back dimensionally changed and finish-compromised.

Hand-stripping with appropriate solvents, scrapers, and detail tools preserves the grain, the profiles, and the structural integrity of the piece. It takes longer. For original historic material, there's no alternative that produces the same result.

This is how Sue has worked for 36 years. Every piece, by hand.

## SECTION: Lead paint in historic homes.

**H2:** Original woodwork in pre-1978 homes has lead paint. EPA certification is required for safe removal.

All original woodwork in homes built before 1978 — which means virtually every historic home in St. Louis's named neighborhoods — has lead paint. Sometimes it's the top coat. Often it's buried under later layers. Either way, sanding, scraping, or stripping without EPA-certified protocols creates lead dust.

Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every project on pre-1978 surfaces is handled with proper containment, HEPA filtration, wet-sanding methods, and documented cleanup. You receive a completion record for your property file.

For historic homes especially, proper lead handling is important. These homes are lived in as primary residences. The woodwork we're restoring is in the rooms where families spend their time.

## SECTION: FAQ

**Q: How do I know if my woodwork can be restored?**
A: Call and describe what you have. Sue can often give you a working read over the phone based on the neighborhood, the age of the house, and what you describe. The free estimate is the real answer — she walks through the spaces, assesses each surface, and tells you honestly what's there and what's possible.

**Q: My woodwork has been painted over. Is it ruined?**
A: Usually not. Painted-over stained woodwork is one of the most common situations in historic St. Louis homes, and most of the time the original wood is intact underneath. The paint has been covering and protecting it. Hand-stripping carefully recovers the original grain and profile. Whether it's fully recoverable depends on the wood species, the paint type, and how many layers there are — we assess that at the estimate.

**Q: Can you match my existing woodwork stain?**
A: Usually. Stain matching on historic woodwork — getting a restored section to read as the same finish as an untouched original — is one of the skills that matters most in this kind of work. We assess the existing tones and get as close as the wood allows. If the original finish has shifted significantly over a century, we'll discuss options before we start.

**Q: How long does historic woodwork restoration take?**
A: It depends on scope. A single door set or room of millwork typically runs 1–3 weeks. A multi-room project covering several spaces may run 4–6 weeks or longer. We build a specific timeline into your estimate.

**Q: What's the difference between restoring original woodwork and replacing it?**
A: Restoration keeps the original material — the specific old-growth wood, the hand-made profiles, the original joinery. Replacement substitutes modern material that approximates the appearance but isn't the same wood, doesn't have the same density, and won't age the same way. For pre-1920s homes in St. Louis's historic neighborhoods, original woodwork that can be restored is almost always worth restoring rather than replacing.
