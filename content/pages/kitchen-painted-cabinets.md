## BODY

### H2: Badly painted cabinets look worse than unpainted ones.

White and grey painted cabinets dominate kitchen design right now — and when they're done correctly, they look clean, current, and timeless. When they're done wrong — brush marks in every door panel, drips at the edges, chipping around the pulls, yellowing within two years — they become the thing you can't stop noticing.

The problem is almost never the paint. It's what happened before the paint went on.

Most painted cabinet jobs fail because the prep was skipped or rushed. Paint applied over an existing stained or painted finish without stripping is only as durable as the weakest layer it's bonded to. When the old finish starts to fail — and it will — the new paint peels right off with it.

### H2: What proper painted cabinet refinishing looks like.

**Strip first, always.** We strip the existing finish back to bare wood before any primer goes on. Not a scuff-sand over the existing coat. A real strip, by hand, so the new primer is bonding to wood — not to a previous finish coat.

**The right primer for the substrate.** Different wood species take primer differently — oak's open grain requires a pore-filling approach before a topcoat can read smooth; maple is denser and more forgiving. Original kitchen cabinetry in St. Louis covers a range of species. We select primer appropriate for what we're working with.

**Finish applied correctly, on a proper cure schedule.** Paint applied too fast over insufficiently cured primer fails early. Each coat is allowed to set properly before the next one goes on.

**EPA Certified for pre-1978 homes.** Kitchen cabinets in homes built before 1978 may have lead paint layered into their finish history — often buried under multiple subsequent coats. Stripping those cabinets requires EPA-certified lead-safe protocols. Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every pre-1978 cabinet project is handled with proper containment and documented cleanup.

### H2: Going from stained to painted — and the reverse.

**Stained to painted** is one of the most common transitions in St. Louis kitchens right now. Many homeowners have original stained cabinets they want to convert to a painted finish. We do this regularly. The process is full strip, appropriate primer for the wood species, then paint. Skipping the strip creates adhesion problems within a few years.

**Painted back to stained** — Some homeowners inherited cabinets that were painted over original stained wood and want to return to the natural finish. This is possible if the original wood surface is intact underneath the paint layers. We strip and assess before committing to a result. In most cases the original wood is fully recoverable.

### H2: Strip & Refinish or Perk Up & Protect?

If your painted cabinets are peeling, chipping, showing brush marks, or were painted over a stained finish — they need **Strip & Refinish**. Full strip, prime, paint. There's no shortcut to a durable result.

If your painted cabinets are in solid condition and holding their finish but looking scuffed or dull — **Perk Up & Protect** may be the right approach. Light prep and a fresh topcoat. We'll tell you honestly which applies when we see them.

**Q: Can you convert my stained cabinets to painted ones?**
A: Yes — it's one of the most common transitions we do. The process is full strip of the existing stain and finish, primer selected for the wood species, then paint. The critical step is the strip: painting over a stained finish without stripping it first creates an adhesion problem that will show up within a few years. Done correctly, the result is clean and durable.

**Q: How is your painted finish different from a DIY paint job?**
A: Prep. A DIY paint job typically scuffs the existing surface and paints over it. That creates a finish that's bonded to the old coat, not to the wood. When the old coat starts to fail — and it will — the paint peels. We strip to bare wood, prime correctly for the species, and apply a cabinet-grade finish on a proper cure schedule. The result bonds to the substrate and holds.

**Q: How do you get a smooth finish without brush marks?**
A: Material selection, application method, and conditions. We use cabinet-grade finishes that flow and level properly. We apply in appropriate conditions — temperature, humidity, and dust affect how paint sets. And we prep the substrate correctly so the finish sits on a stable, even surface. Brush marks in a finished cabinet are almost always a prep or product problem.

**Q: What kind of paint do you use on cabinets?**
A: A polyurethane-based cabinet finish — oil or water-based depending on the application and the substrate. We don't use wall paint on cabinetry. Kitchen surfaces need a finish that handles repeated contact, cleaning products, heat cycling, and humidity. They require the right product for the surface.

**Q: My cabinets were painted by a previous owner. Can you fix them?**
A: Usually. This is very common in older St. Louis homes — original stained cabinets that were painted at some point, often without proper prep. We strip back to bare wood and start clean. Sometimes there are surprises underneath (multiple layers, filler, damage). We tell you what we find before we proceed.
