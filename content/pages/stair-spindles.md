## BODY

### H2: The most labor-intensive surface on any staircase.

Spindles are the most time-consuming component of staircase refinishing — and the one that reveals most clearly whether the work was done correctly. A run of 30 turned balusters, each with its own profile, each needing to be stripped, prepped, and finished on all sides: there are no shortcuts that produce a good result.

Chemical dip-stripping destroys spindle work. Submerging turned balusters in a stripping tank attacks the glue joints where spindles meet the treads and handrail. Joints loosen. Profiles soften. The wood absorbs moisture unevenly and the finish that goes on afterward looks wrong.

We strip each spindle by hand — detail tools, appropriate solvents, patience. The profiles stay sharp. The joints stay tight. The finished result looks like what it is: original woodwork that's been properly cared for.

### H2: Original turned spindles are irreplaceable.

In St. Louis's historic homes — Craftsman bungalows, late-Victorian two-stories, Edwardian foursquares — the turned spindles date from roughly 1890 to 1920 and were made from old-growth wood on production lathes that don't exist anymore. The profiles are tight, varied, and specific to the design of the staircase they're in.

Modern replacement balusters are machine-produced from pine or poplar in a handful of generic profiles. They don't read the same. They don't have the same density or grain character. If the original spindles are present and structurally sound, refinishing them is always the better choice.

### H2: Mismatched spindles — a common problem we solve.

Many St. Louis staircases have been patched over the years: a few spindles were replaced with a different profile, or different sections were refinished at different times and have drifted apart in color. The result is a staircase that looks off without the homeowner quite knowing why.

We treat the full spindle run as a unit. Stain selection, application, and finish sheen are consistent across all pieces. Where profiles differ due to prior repairs, we work to create visual harmony across the run. It reads as a cohesive staircase, not a patchwork.

### H2: Painted spindles, stained spindles — both are in our scope.

Many St. Louis staircases have painted spindles against a stained handrail and treads. In late 19th and early 20th century construction, this was architecturally standard for a number of styles and it's a legitimate historic look. We refinish to whatever the original or desired specification is: painted white, painted black, or stained to match the rail.

If spindles were painted over original stain and you want to return to stained wood, we strip and assess what's underneath before committing to a result. The original wood surface in most cases is perfectly intact.

### H2: Perk Up & Protect — for spindles that don't need a full strip.

If your spindles are in good structural condition and still holding their finish — just looking dull, dinged, or faded — a **Perk Up & Protect** maintenance coat may be the right approach. Light preparation and a fresh topcoat restore the appearance without the time and cost of a full Strip & Refinish. We'll tell you honestly which your spindles need.

### H2: EPA Certified for pre-1978 staircases.

Painted spindles in homes built before 1978 contain lead paint. Stripping them generates lead dust in the main circulation area of your home. Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator — every pre-1978 spindle project is handled with proper containment, HEPA filtration, and documented cleanup.

## FAQ BLOCK

**Q: Do all my spindles need to match each other?**
A: Visually, yes — and we treat the full run as a unit for exactly that reason. Spindles that were refinished at different times, or replaced with different profiles, contribute to a staircase that looks inconsistent. Doing them all together at one time with the same stain and finish produces a result that reads cohesively. If profiles differ across the run due to prior repairs, we work within that constraint to create as much visual harmony as possible.

**Q: Can you strip painted spindles without removing them from the staircase?**
A: Yes. Spindles are stripped in place — removing them would require disassembling the staircase and risks damage to the joints. We use hand tools and appropriate solvents to strip each spindle individually while it remains installed. This is standard for all our spindle work.

**Q: How long does spindle work take for a typical staircase?**
A: For a standard single-flight staircase with 20–35 spindles, plan on 3–5 days of working time from strip to finished topcoat. Larger staircases with more spindles — a main stair plus upper landing, or staircases with multiple decorative profiles — take proportionally longer. We quote timing at the estimate stage.

**Q: My spindles are painted white but I want them stained. Is that possible?**
A: It depends on what's underneath. If the original wood surface is intact, stripping to bare wood and staining is usually achievable. If there's significant filler or surface damage under the paint layers, the result may not be what you're expecting. We strip and assess before committing.

**Q: Why is spindle refinishing priced per spindle rather than by the staircase?**
A: Because the labor is per spindle. A tread is a flat surface finished on one face. A turned spindle has multiple profiles, needs to be finished on all sides, and requires careful detail work throughout. The time per spindle is significantly higher than the time per tread. Pricing reflects time, not materials.
