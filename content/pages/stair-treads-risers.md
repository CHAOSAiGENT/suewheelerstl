## BODY

### H2: What worn-through treads actually mean.

The center of a stair tread — the landing strip where every foot falls — is where the finish fails first. You notice it as a dull, lighter-colored band across the middle of each step. That's not just a cosmetic issue. Once the topcoat wears through, every step grinds foot traffic directly into bare wood. The wood darkens from oils and dirt. It abrades. The damage compounds with every use.

Left unaddressed, worn-through treads don't stay stable. The bare wood picks up moisture, the edges of the tread swell slightly, and the uneven surface creates a tripping hazard — subtle at first, then progressive. Refinishing a tread in poor but not yet dangerous condition is straightforward. Refinishing one that's been bare wood for years is harder.

The time to address worn treads is before they reach bare wood, not after.

### H2: Finish selection matters more on treads than anywhere else.

The polyurethane we use on a stair tread is not the same product we use on a wall paneling or a door. Tread finish has to perform under repeated impact, abrasion, foot oils, and cleaning — with a sheen level that doesn't show every scuff but provides real surface hardness.

We use oil- or water-based polyurethane selected specifically for high-traffic horizontal surfaces. The sheen is typically satin to semi-gloss — high gloss shows every mark, flat sheens don't build adequate hardness. The prep matters too: a tread stripped to bare wood and sanded to the correct profile holds finish significantly longer than one that received a coat over an existing degraded base.

This is one of the surfaces where shortcuts in prep create visible failure within a year or two. We don't take shortcuts here.

### H2: Treads and risers — why both at once makes sense.

Treads and risers are different surfaces with different wear rates. Treads take constant foot traffic; risers — the vertical face of each step — typically hold finish much longer. It's common to have treads that need full Strip & Refinish while the risers just need a light sand and fresh topcoat.

That said, there's a strong visual case for doing them together. A freshly refinished tread against an unaddressed riser creates an immediate contrast — the new finish against the old makes the riser look worse than it did before. We typically quote treads and risers as a unit and discuss the condition of each. For risers in decent shape, a **Perk Up & Protect** maintenance coat alongside a full tread Strip & Refinish is often the right answer.

### H2: Matching treads to your hardwood floors — how it works.

One of the most common asks on a stair tread project is matching the tread finish to the adjacent hardwood floor. It's a reasonable goal, and it's usually achievable — with some realistic expectations.

If your floors were refinished recently and you know the stain, that's the easiest scenario. We can match it closely. If your floors have aged significantly — ten or fifteen years since their last refinish — the color has shifted, and we're trying to hit a moving target. Stair treads and floors are also often different species: red oak versus white oak, maple versus pine, each of which absorbs stain differently.

We assess both surfaces, test stain samples where appropriate, and give you a realistic picture of how close we can get before we start. In most cases, we land in a range that reads as matched from a normal viewing distance. We'll tell you honestly when an exact match isn't achievable.

### H2: Is this a Strip & Refinish or a Perk Up & Protect situation?

**Strip & Refinish** is the right call for treads when:

- The finish has worn through to bare wood anywhere on the tread
- The surface has dark staining, embedded dirt, or visible water marks
- The existing finish is peeling or bubbling
- The treads have never been refinished and the original finish is decades old

**Perk Up & Protect** is the right call when:

- The finish is intact but dull, scratched, or faded uniformly
- There's no bare wood exposure
- You want to extend the life of a recent refinish without a full strip

When in doubt, we assess during the estimate and tell you which approach is right for your stairs.

**Q: Can you refinish just the treads without doing the whole staircase?**
A: Yes. Tread-only projects are common — treads wear significantly faster than spindles or railings. The tread finish won't match un-refinished components perfectly, but it's a practical approach when budget or timing makes a full staircase refinish premature. We'll discuss the visual tradeoffs honestly.

**Q: Can you match my treads to my hardwood floors?**
A: In most cases, yes — close enough to read as matched from a normal viewing distance. The variables are wood species, how long ago the floors were refinished, and how much the floor color has shifted since then. We assess both surfaces before staining and give you a realistic expectation.

**Q: How long until we can walk on the stairs after refinishing?**
A: Light foot traffic is typically possible within 24–48 hours. Full cure for heavy use takes longer — usually 5–7 days for the topcoat to reach full hardness. We give you specific timing based on the products used and the conditions during cure.

**Q: My treads have some gouges and worn spots in the center. Can those be repaired?**
A: Minor surface damage — center-of-tread wear, small gouges, surface scratches — is addressed as part of a standard Strip & Refinish: filled, sanded flush, finished. Deep structural cracks or damage through the tread are assessed individually and we'll tell you if any need to be replaced rather than refinished.

**Q: How long will refinished treads last?**
A: With quality polyurethane and proper prep, 10–15 years before the finish needs significant attention is realistic under normal household use. Pets, high heels, and heavy traffic accelerate wear. A **Perk Up & Protect** maintenance coat partway through the cycle extends the life of a full refinish significantly — and costs less than doing a full Strip & Refinish again.
