## SECTION: Why Staircase Work Is Different

**H2:** The most labor-intensive refinishing we do. _Also the highest-return._

Staircase refinishing is demanding work. Here's why — and why it's worth it.

**The spindles.** Most staircases have 30 to 80 or more individual spindles. Each one has to be stripped by hand, sanded, and finished. A dip tank dissolves the glue joints at the base — spindles come loose and the profiles are ruined. There is no machine shortcut. Hand-strip, hand-sand, hand-finish. That's what it takes and that's what we do.

**The treads.** Stair treads are the highest-traffic wood surface in your home. They need a finish built for that abuse — multiple coats of polyurethane, properly cured, on a correctly prepped surface. A thin coat over inadequate prep fails in two years. We don't do that.

**The newel post.** In pre-1920 St. Louis homes, newel posts were often turned or carved from old-growth hardwood. They are architectural statements — the exclamation point at the bottom of the staircase. Refinishing one correctly means stripping carefully to preserve every detail, then finishing to bring out the depth of the wood rather than flatten it.

**The geometry.** Nothing on a staircase is flat. Every surface is angled, curved, or in a tight corner. Good refinishing work on a staircase takes patience, the right tools, and experience with the form. We've done hundreds of them in St. Louis homes.

## SECTION: Service Options

**H2:** Strip & Refinish or _Perk Up & Protect?_

**Strip & Refinish (S&R)**
Full restoration. Strip all components to bare wood, repair damage, stain if needed, finish with polyurethane. For staircases with worn treads, failing finish on spindles, or woodwork that needs a complete color correction. Most staircases that call for a project call for this.

**Perk Up & Protect (PUP)**
Maintenance coat. Light scuff-sand and fresh finish over a sound existing base. For staircases in good structural condition but looking dull or lightly worn. Less disruption, faster, less expensive — but not right for every staircase. We'll tell you honestly which applies to yours.

## SECTION: Lead Paint

**H2:** Pre-1978 staircase? _EPA certification matters._

Stair railings, spindles, and newel posts in homes built before 1978 typically have lead paint in the existing finish layers. Stripping these surfaces without EPA-certified lead-safe protocols generates lead dust in your home.

Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Lead-safe work on pre-1978 staircases is standard practice — documented, contained, and correctly disposed of. You receive a completion record.

## SECTION: FAQ BLOCK

_Accordion — FAQ schema markup_

**Q: How long does staircase refinishing take?**
A: A full staircase with treads, risers, spindles, newel post, and railings typically takes 2–4 weeks. Tread-and-riser-only projects without spindle work are faster. We give you a realistic timeline with your estimate.

**Q: Can you refinish just the treads and leave the spindles?**
A: Yes. We work on whatever components need attention. If the treads are worn but the spindles are sound, we can address the treads selectively. We'll tell you honestly whether that makes visual and practical sense for your specific staircase.

**Q: Can spindles be dipped to speed things up?**
A: No. Dipping dissolves the glue at the base of turned spindles, loosens them from the tread and rail, and destroys the original profile. There is no shortcut for spindle stripping. We do it by hand.

**Q: My staircase has carved newel posts. Can you preserve the detail?**
A: Yes — that's exactly what hand-stripping is for. We don't use methods that soften or blur carved profiles. Every detail in the original wood stays sharp.

**Q: Do I need to avoid using the staircase during refinishing?**
A: For part of the project, yes — particularly during finish application and cure. We plan the work to minimize disruption. For homes with a single staircase, we stage the work so you can use it within a day or two after the final coat, even if full cure takes longer.

**Q: My staircase doesn't match my new hardwood floors. Can you fix that?**
A: Yes. This is one of the most common staircase calls we get. We stain and finish the staircase to match your new floors, assessing both surfaces and working to match as closely as the wood allows.

**Q: What if parts of the staircase have been repaired or replaced and don't match?**
A: This is common in historic homes — repairs done over decades in mismatched stains. Part of what we do is assess the full scope and develop a finishing plan that brings the staircase to a unified look. It's not always possible to get a perfect match between old and new wood, but we can get it close and we'll tell you what to expect before we start.
