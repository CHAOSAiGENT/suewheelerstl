## BODY

### H2: Why handrails fail first.

The handrail is the highest-contact wood surface in a home, and it fails in a distinctive way. The top of the rail — the grip area — wears through from constant hand contact. The finish picks up oils from skin, cleaning products, and everyday handling in a way that floor or millwork finishes don't experience. The profile edges peel where the topcoat can't flex with the wood movement.

A handrail finish failure isn't just a cosmetic problem. A tacky, rough, or peeling handrail is the one you grab every time you use the stairs. It looks neglected in photographs — buyers notice it immediately in listing photos before they see anything else about the staircase. And a worn, compromised rail finish that's been left to deteriorate is significantly harder to restore cleanly than one caught at the early wear stage.

The right time to address a handrail is before it's worn through to bare wood.

### H2: Railing, spindles, and newel — understanding the system.

A stair balustrade is a system: the **handrail** (the piece you grip), the **balusters or spindles** (the vertical members between rail and tread), and the **newel posts** (the anchor posts at the top and bottom of the run, often more heavily detailed). Each component can be in different condition — and often is.

It's common to have a handrail that's worn bare at the grip while the spindles are intact but painted, and a newel post that's in decent shape but a different finish tone than either. We assess each component separately and discuss what makes sense to do together versus in phases.

**What we refinish in this category:**

- Continuous handrail (full length, including profile, transitions, and returns)
- Newel posts (bottom post, landing posts, top post)
- Balusters and spindles — wood only; see note on wrought iron below

### H2: Railing-only projects — when they make sense.

Most railing work happens as part of a full staircase project. But railing-only refinishing is a legitimate scope when:

- The handrail is significantly more worn than the rest of the staircase
- You're addressing the staircase in phases over time
- The treads were recently refinished but the railing wasn't done at the same time

When the railing is done standalone, the finish won't match the un-refinished components exactly — the new finish on the handrail will look different against older finish on spindles or treads. We'll tell you what that looks like before you commit to the scope.

### H2: Railing color and the staircase as a design decision.

Railing and newel finishes don't have to match the treads — and frequently shouldn't. In many St. Louis historic homes, the original design intention was a darker or more contrasting railing against a lighter tread finish. It's an intentional design element that defines the staircase visually.

If you're doing a full staircase refinish, the color relationship between tread and railing is worth talking through. Most clients want the staircase to look "right" — consistent and intentional — but "right" doesn't always mean everything matching. We bring that conversation to the estimate and can show you examples from comparable work.

### H2: Painted spindles — getting back to wood.

In many St. Louis homes, the balusters were painted at some point — often at the same time as the walls — while the treads and newel were left stained. This is a common mixed-state situation, and it's resolvable.

Stripping paint from spindles is more labor-intensive than stripping a flat surface. Spindles have profiles, turned sections, and narrow faces that require detail work. We assess the spindle count, profile complexity, and paint history before quoting. In most cases, solid wood original balusters are worth stripping — they're irreplaceable, and the result is significantly better than painted spindles next to a stained newel and rail.

### H2: Wrought iron or mixed-material balustrades.

If your staircase has wrought iron balusters alongside a wood handrail and newel post — common in mid-century and contemporary St. Louis homes — the scope is different. We refinish the wood components: the handrail, the newel post, and any wood cap rails or transitions. We don't paint or refinish wrought iron. If the iron needs work, that's a separate scope by a different contractor, and we can help you think through the sequencing.

## FAQ BLOCK

**Q: Can you refinish just the handrail without doing the spindles?**
A: Yes. Handrail-only projects are common when the rail is significantly more worn than the rest of the staircase. The new finish on the rail won't match the existing finish on spindles exactly — we'll tell you what that contrast will look like before you commit to the scope.

**Q: My railing has a profile — not flat on top. Can you strip and refinish the profile without losing the detail?**
A: Yes. Profile work on handrails is standard. We use appropriate detail tools to strip the profile correctly without rounding the edges or obscuring the geometry. Profile preservation is a point of care, not an afterthought.

**Q: My railing is painted over original stain. Can you get back to the wood?**
A: Usually yes. Painted rails in historic homes are common — often painted over at the same time as the spindles, while the treads stayed stained. We strip and assess what's underneath. Original stain finish is recoverable in most cases. Heavily filled surfaces or rails with significant damage are assessed individually.

**Q: Can you match my railing stain to my treads?**
A: Yes — and we'd discuss whether that's the right call. Many historic staircases had an intentional contrast between the railing/newel color and the tread color. We can match them if that's the goal, or we can help you work toward a relationship between the two finishes that looks intentional and appropriate for the house.

**Q: What about a wrought iron balustrade with a wood handrail?**
A: We refinish the wood components — handrail, newel, any wood rails or transitions. Wrought iron is outside our scope. If the iron needs repainting, that's a separate contractor. We can help you think through which should happen first.

**Q: How long does a handrail refinish hold up?**
A: With quality polyurethane selected for hand contact, a refinished handrail should look good for 8–12 years with normal household use. Heavily-used stairs with multiple residents accelerate wear at the grip area. A **Perk Up & Protect** maintenance coat at the midpoint of the cycle extends the finish life significantly without a full strip.
