## SECTION: Shaw homes and their woodwork

Shaw sits along the northern edge of the Missouri Botanical Garden — one of the reasons the neighborhood has attracted longtime homeowners who care about quality and permanence. The housing stock built between 1890 and 1915 is primarily brick Victorian and Craftsman bungalow, with the latter becoming the dominant style after 1905.

What distinguishes Shaw's Craftsman woodwork is the emphasis on stained rather than painted wood. Craftsman design philosophy held that natural wood grain was decorative in itself — so the oak and fir in these homes was meant to be seen. Interior doors with simple raised panels, stained and varnished. Built-in buffets and china hutches in the dining room, finished to show the grain. Staircases with clean spindle work and solid square newel posts. Baseboards and door surrounds with a heavier Craftsman profile.

The problem is that somewhere between the 1950s and 1980s, a lot of this stained woodwork got painted over. Painters found it easier than stripping and re-staining. The result is that Shaw homes are full of wood that looks like painted trim but is actually original Craftsman oak — waiting.

## SECTION: What we refinish in Shaw

Shaw projects often begin with a homeowner who suspects there's something worth having under the paint, and end with them discovering they were right.

**Built-in buffets and china hutches.** The dining room built-in is Shaw's equivalent of the CWE pocket door — it's in nearly every single-family home and it's almost always original. Most are solid white oak or chestnut, frequently with quarter-sawing that creates a distinctive ray-fleck pattern. We strip them by hand, repair any profile damage, and finish to restore the original stained character. The result reads as original furniture-quality woodwork rather than painted trim.

**Interior doors.** Shaw's Craftsman interior doors — typically two-panel or three-panel with simple flat or slightly raised panels — were meant to be stained, not painted. We remove them, strip by hand in the shop, and refinish in a stain that matches or complements the surrounding woodwork. Doors that looked like painted hollow-core will look like what they actually are: solid old-growth wood.

**Staircases.** The staircases in Shaw's single-family homes tend toward clean Craftsman design — square or simply turned spindles, box newel posts, straight railings. Simpler than the Victorian flourishes in neighboring Lafayette Square, but solid and well-proportioned. We do the full scope: treads, risers, spindles, newel post, and railing — all stripped and restained together so the result is consistent.

**Architectural woodwork and Craftsman trim.** Shaw's Craftsman homes have a particular trim profile — flatter and wider than Victorian, with minimal ornament but substantial proportions. Door surrounds, baseboards, and window casings in these homes are typically solid wood and worth restoring rather than replacing.

## SECTION: Restoring original woodwork vs. replacing it

The Craftsman oak in a Shaw home from 1908 has properties that contemporary material simply doesn't. Old-growth white oak is dense, stable, and has a grain figure — especially in quarter-sawn form — that modern lumber lacks. When it's been painted over, it looks like any other painted surface. When it's stripped and properly finished, it's clear why the original builders used it.

Replacement means installing contemporary lumber milled from fast-grown trees that are less stable, less dense, and less visually interesting. It means finding a profile that matches the original — which is possible but requires custom millwork. And it means spending more for a result that is objectively inferior to what you already have.

Restoration is the better answer. We've done it in hundreds of Shaw homes.

## SECTION: Lead paint and pre-1978 homes in Shaw

Shaw's housing stock almost entirely predates 1978. Many homes have original stained woodwork under multiple layers of paint — each layer potentially lead-containing. Stripping without proper precautions is a health hazard.

Sue Wheeler is EPA Certified under the RRP rule. Containment, HEPA vacuuming, and documentation are standard on every Shaw project. This protects your household during the work and ensures the project is legally compliant.

## SECTION: FAQ

**Q: How do I know if my Shaw home's woodwork has been painted over vs. always painted?**
The easiest tell is the edge condition. Craftsman-era stained wood has a different visual weight than wood that was always painted — and at door hinges, drawer pulls, and trim corners, you can often see the original stained layer beneath the paint. We check at the estimate and can give you a confident answer before any work begins.

**Q: Can you match the original Craftsman stain color?**
We can get very close. Original Craftsman stain was typically a medium-to-dark golden oak or walnut tone. We test stain samples on a small area before committing to the full piece and adjust until the result is right.

**Q: I just want to refresh the woodwork that's already in good condition. Do you do that?**
Yes. We offer a maintenance coat — what we call Perk Up & Protect — for wood that's in good shape but looking dull or worn. It's a lighter scope than full strip-and-refinish, and it's a good option for woodwork that's been well cared for and just needs attention.

**Q: Do you work in Shaw's two-flats as well as single-family homes?**
Yes. Two-flat work is common in Shaw — typically doors and staircases. The scope is similar to single-family work.

**Q: My neighbors had their cabinets painted white instead of stripped. Can I still have mine stripped?**
Yes. Stripping painted-over stained woodwork is exactly what we do. The choices your neighbors made don't affect your project.
