## SECTION: What is architectural woodwork?

**H2:** Every piece of wood that defines the shape and character of a room.

Architectural woodwork is all the in-place wood that frames and finishes a room's surfaces. It's not furniture — it doesn't move. It's not structural lumber — you don't see it in the walls. It's the layer between the structure and the living space: the trim, the molding, the framing, the built-in cabinetry that was designed for the house.

**What counts as architectural woodwork:**

- Door frames and door casings
- Window frames and window trim
- Baseboards and base caps
- Crown molding and cornice trim
- Chair rail and picture rail
- Wainscoting and paneling
- Built-in bookcases and library walls
- Built-in hutches, buffets, and china cabinets
- Fireplace surrounds and overmantels (mantels)
- Coffered ceiling beams and panels
- Decorative columns and pilasters
- Pocket door housing and surrounds
- Butler's pantry cabinetry
- Staircase newel posts, handrails, and balustrades

If it was built into the house and you can see it from the room, it's architectural woodwork.

What we don't refinish: freestanding furniture. A built-in hutch framed into the dining room wall is architectural woodwork. A freestanding china cabinet that sits against the same wall is furniture. We focus on the former.

## SECTION: Why architectural woodwork is worth restoring.

**H2:** The original millwork in a pre-1920s St. Louis home is irreplaceable. Here's what's actually at stake.

Most of the architectural woodwork in St. Louis's older neighborhoods was installed between 1880 and 1930. That woodwork was made from old-growth lumber — slower-growing trees with tighter grain and greater density than anything grown commercially today. White oak, American chestnut, heart pine, old-growth fir. Dense enough to hold a sharp profile. Stable enough not to move much with humidity changes. Hard enough that finish lasts decades.

Modern reproduction millwork uses different wood and different methods. The profiles may look similar. The behavior isn't. Fast-grown wood with wider grain doesn't take stain the same way, doesn't hold finish as long, and doesn't have the visual depth of old-growth material. You can see the difference if you know what you're looking at.

Replacing original architectural woodwork doesn't upgrade the house. It removes something that can't be put back.

Refinishing — stripping the original finish, addressing any damage, and applying a new finish to the original wood — is the only path that keeps what's actually there. The wood stays. The profiles stay. The character stays.

## SECTION: How architectural woodwork projects are scoped.

**H2:** We bid room by room, item by item. Here's what that means.

Architectural woodwork isn't priced as a single job — it's assessed surface by surface, room by room. The condition of each element matters: crown molding in one room may be in fine shape and a candidate for a maintenance coat, while the wainscoting in the same room has failing finish and needs a full strip.

At the estimate, we walk through the spaces you want addressed. We assess each surface: finish condition, wood species, any damage, lead paint presence (in pre-1978 homes, it's almost always there). The quote breaks down by room and element so you know exactly what each piece of the project costs.

You can take on the whole house at once or start with a single room. Most clients start with the room where the woodwork is most visible — the dining room, the entryway, the living room — and add rooms from there.

## SECTION: Full refinish or Perk Up & Protect?

**H2:** Some surfaces need a full strip. Some just need a refresh.

**Strip & Refinish** is a complete restoration. Existing finish stripped to bare wood, repairs addressed, new stain if applicable, new polyurethane topcoat. For architectural woodwork with failing finish, significant paint buildup, or surfaces that haven't been properly finished in 30 or 40 years. Most of the woodwork we see in historic St. Louis homes needs this.

**Perk Up & Protect (PUP)** is a maintenance coat. Wet sand to level light surface damage, color refresh if needed, fresh topcoat over a solid existing base. No chemical stripping. For woodwork that's structurally sound and holding its finish but looking dull or lightly worn. Faster, less expensive, less disruption.

Some rooms get both — a section of wainscoting that's in decent shape gets PUP while a built-in bookcase with peeling finish gets a full Strip & Refinish. We scope each element honestly and tell you what it needs.

## SECTION: Lead paint and pre-1978 homes.

**H2:** Pre-1920s architectural woodwork almost certainly has lead paint. We handle it correctly.

Virtually all original architectural woodwork in St. Louis homes built before 1940 has lead paint — often multiple layers of it. In kitchens and bathrooms it's obvious. In woodwork throughout the living spaces it's usually buried under decades of subsequent paint.

Stripping lead-painted woodwork without EPA-certified protocols creates lead dust in living spaces. This matters especially for architectural woodwork because it's in the rooms where your family spends the most time.

Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every project on pre-1978 surfaces is handled with proper containment, HEPA filtration, wet-sanding methods, and documented cleanup. You receive a completion record.

## SECTION: FAQ

**Q: What's included in architectural woodwork refinishing?**
A: Any in-place wood that frames or finishes the room's surfaces — door frames, window trim, baseboards, crown molding, chair rail, wainscoting, built-in cabinetry, mantels, decorative columns, pocket door housings. If it was built into the house and you can see it from the room, it's in scope.

**Q: How is architectural woodwork different from furniture?**
A: Architectural woodwork is attached to the house — built in, framed in, or installed as part of the room. Furniture is freestanding. We specialize in architectural woodwork: the pieces that define the character of the room because they were designed as part of it. For freestanding furniture, we're not the right fit.

**Q: How do you price architectural woodwork jobs?**
A: Room by room, surface by surface. At the estimate, we walk through the spaces and assess each element — crown molding, wainscoting, door casings, any built-ins. The quote itemizes each room so you know exactly what you're getting. You can start with one room or take on several at once.

**Q: Can you work on woodwork that's been painted over multiple times?**
A: Yes. Painted-over original woodwork is one of the most common situations we see in pre-WWII homes. Sometimes the layers are thick enough that a room's original profiles are partially obscured. Hand-stripping carefully recovers the original geometry. We'll assess what's recoverable at the estimate.

**Q: How long does an architectural woodwork project take?**
A: It depends on scope. A single room with wainscoting and crown molding typically runs 1–2 weeks. A full-floor project covering several rooms may run 3–4 weeks or longer. We give you a specific timeline with your estimate.
