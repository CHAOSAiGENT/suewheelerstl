## BODY

### H2: The problem with piecemeal interior door history.

In most older St. Louis homes, the interior doors have a complicated history. Some are original — solid wood, beautiful profiles, finished to match the millwork when the house was built. Some have been painted over at some point, usually during a renovation that prioritized speed over preservation. A few may have been replaced entirely with hollow-core doors that don't belong in the room.

The result is a house that never quite reads as finished. The doors are all slightly different — different sheens, different undertones, different levels of wear. It's not obvious what's wrong, but it's felt. Rooms that should feel pulled-together feel slightly off.

Fixing it isn't complicated. It's a question of stripping to bare wood, matching to the surrounding millwork as closely as the species and grain allow, and finishing everything to a consistent standard. Door by door, or the whole set.

### H2: Getting under the paint — what's usually there.

In Central West End, Benton Park, Compton Heights, and Shaw, the original interior woodwork on pre-1940 homes is almost always worth uncovering. Original doors in these homes are solid wood — often old-growth fir, oak, or chestnut — with panel profiles and profiles on the casing that hollow-core replacements can't replicate.

When paint has been applied over original stain, the question is always: what's the condition of the original finish underneath? The answer depends on how many paint layers are present, how well the paint adhered, and whether the original finish was varnish or shellac (which strips cleanly) or a heavier oil-based paint (which takes more work to separate).

We strip and assess before committing to a result. If the original finish is intact and recoverable, we take it there. If the paint history has compromised the surface, we tell you what the realistic options are — which may include a fresh stain to a color you choose, to match the trim or set a new direction.

### H2: Stain matching — what's achievable and what isn't.

Stain matching is one of the most common asks on interior door projects. The goal is usually to make the doors read with the floor, the trim, or both.

Here's what's realistic: we can get very close. Wood species, grain porosity, and decades of color shift in existing finishes all affect the final result. Floors and door trim are often different species — red oak versus white oak, fir versus pine — which absorbs stain differently. The goal is visual harmony, not a laboratory match.

For a room where the doors, casings, and baseboards are all the same species, we can match within a range that's indistinguishable from a normal distance. For floors that have aged twenty years since their last refinish, we're trying to match a moving target — and we'll tell you honestly how close we can get before we start.

### H2: The board-up method for interior doors.

Interior door projects use the same board-up approach as exterior work: door comes off the hinges, goes to the shop, comes back refinished and rehung. Controlled temperature and humidity during the cure phase produce better results than on-site finishing in a room.

For multi-door sets — four, six, or eight doors in a house — we stage the work so you're never without access to all rooms at the same time. Doors come back one or two at a time, all finished to the same standard.

For doors that can't be removed — built-in configuration, unusual hinge placement, or a door you simply don't want to unmount — on-site work is available on a case-by-case basis.

### H2: EPA Certified for pre-1978 interior doors.

Lead paint isn't just an exterior concern. Interior doors in St. Louis homes built before 1978 frequently have lead-containing finish somewhere in their history — sometimes visible as paint, sometimes buried under subsequent work. It's especially common in bedrooms and in rooms where original decorating was done before modern standards.

Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every pre-1978 interior door project includes proper containment, HEPA filtration, and documented cleanup. This is required by law for renovation work in pre-1978 homes — and it's standard practice here regardless.

**Q: Can you match my door stain to my hardwood floors?**
A: We aim to get as close as possible — and usually land within a range that reads as matched from normal viewing distance. The technical challenge is that doors and floors are often different wood species with different grain and porosity, and original floor finishes have shifted color over decades. We assess both surfaces and give you a realistic expectation before we start.

**Q: My interior doors were painted over original stain. Can you get back to the wood?**
A: Usually yes. It depends on how many layers of paint are present and the condition of what's underneath. We strip and assess before committing to a result. If the original stain finish is intact, we take it back there. If it's been compromised, we'll discuss what the realistic options are — including a fresh stain application that coordinates with the rest of the room.

**Q: I have eight interior doors. Can you do them all at once?**
A: Yes, and it's often the most efficient approach. We stage the work so you always have access to the rooms you need. All doors come back finished to the same standard at the same time, so your millwork reads as a consistent set. Multi-door projects also typically cost less per door than single-door jobs.

**Q: Should I refinish all my interior doors or just the ones that look worst?**
A: It depends on how inconsistent they currently are. If the house has some original stained doors and some painted-over ones, doing the painted ones to match makes sense as a project. If they're all original and just worn, doing the worst ones first is a reasonable approach — with the understanding that the newly refinished doors will look different than the ones still waiting. We'll help you think through the right sequencing for your situation.

**Q: How long does interior door refinishing take?**
A: A single door returns in 3–5 days. Larger sets are staged and typically completed within one to two weeks depending on count. We give you a specific timeline with the estimate.
