## BODY

### H2: You don't need new cabinets. You need a new color.

If your kitchen cabinet boxes are structurally sound — doors hang correctly, drawers work, joints are tight — the cabinets themselves aren't the problem. What you want to change is the finish.

Cabinet color-change refinishing does exactly that. We strip the existing finish, prepare the surface for the new color, and apply whatever you want — a lighter stain, a richer stain, a contemporary grey tone, or a clean painted finish. The boxes stay. The kitchen looks completely different.

New kitchen cabinets in a typical St. Louis home cost $20,000–$50,000 or more depending on scope, species, and installation. A color-change refinishing project costs a fraction of that and produces a result that reads as a fully updated kitchen to anyone who walks in.

### H2: The three color-change scenarios — and why prep differs for each.

**Stain to different stain.** The most common request. Moving from 1980s or 90s honey-oak to a richer walnut, espresso, or grey-toned stain. The wood grain stays visible; the color shifts entirely. For darker-to-lighter transitions, full strip is required — you cannot stain over a darker stain and get a clean lighter result. For darker transitions, there's more flexibility, but we always recommend stripping first for a durable, even result.

**Stain to paint.** Converting original stained cabinets to a painted finish. Full strip, appropriate primer for the wood species, then paint. The strip is not optional here: painting over a stained finish without removing it creates an adhesion failure that will show up within a few years.

**Paint to different paint.** Existing painted cabinets, new color. Strip the old paint, reprime, repaint. We don't coat over the existing paint — we strip to bare wood so the new color bonds to the substrate and not to a failing previous coat.

### H2: What the process looks like in your kitchen.

Doors and drawer fronts come off the cabinets and are worked on flat — this is how you get a finish without runs, drips, or brush marks on vertical surfaces. Cabinet frames are addressed in place. Everything is reassembled and rehung after finish and cure.

We discuss timing at the estimate: how many days the kitchen will be inaccessible, what to plan for sequencing, and what to expect at each stage. There are no surprises.

### H2: Color selection — Sue can help.

Stain selection for a color-change project is more involved than it looks. The target color on a sample board and the actual result on your specific wood species, in your specific kitchen light, can differ. We test stain on an inconspicuous surface before committing to the full project. If you're trying to coordinate with existing hardwood floors or trim, we assess those tones and advise.

Painted finishes give more predictability in color — the grain doesn't influence the final read the way it does with stain. But primer selection still matters for the substrate, and we select appropriately.

### H2: EPA Certified for pre-1978 kitchens.

Pre-1978 kitchen cabinets frequently have lead paint somewhere in their finish history — sometimes buried under multiple subsequent coats. Stripping them for a color change requires EPA-certified lead-safe protocols. Sue Wheeler is an EPA Certified Lead Renovator. Every pre-1978 project is handled with proper containment and documented cleanup.

**Q: Can I go from dark stain to light stain on my oak cabinets?**
A: Yes, but it requires a full strip first. You cannot stain over a darker stain and get a clean lighter result — the old color bleeds through. We strip to bare wood, which also gives us the opportunity to neutralize the orange undertones that are common in 1980s and 90s oak. Then we apply the new lighter stain. The result is a color change that reads clean and intentional.

**Q: Can I go from stained wood to painted cabinets?**
A: Yes. It's one of the most common transitions we do. Full strip of the stained finish, primer selected for the wood species, then paint. The important step is the strip — skipping it and painting over the stain directly creates adhesion problems that show up within a few years. Done correctly, painted-over-stained cabinets look clean and hold well.

**Q: What if I change my mind about the color after we start?**
A: We test stain samples on an inconspicuous surface before committing to the full project, specifically to avoid this. Color direction is confirmed before any significant work proceeds. After stripping and staining has begun, changing course requires additional work and time — we'll discuss options and be direct about what's involved.

**Q: Will updating my cabinet color affect my home's resale value?**
A: Usually positively. Updated kitchen cabinet finishes — particularly moving from dated honey-oak or dark-stain to contemporary neutrals — is consistently cited by St. Louis real estate agents as a high-ROI pre-sale improvement. The specific impact depends on the neighborhood and buyer expectations in that market.

**Q: How long does a color-change project take?**
A: For an average kitchen, plan on 5–10 business days from strip to final cure. Stain-to-paint transitions take slightly longer due to additional primer stages. We discuss timing at the estimate.
