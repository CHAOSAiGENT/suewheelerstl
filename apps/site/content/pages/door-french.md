## BODY

### H2: Why French doors fail faster — and what to do about it.

A French door has more surface complexity than almost any other door type. The wood-to-glass transitions — every muntin, every glass edge, every glazing channel — are where moisture infiltrates first when the finish starts to fail. Water migrates behind the glazing compound. Wood swells. The finish lifts. The glass seal can be compromised.

The same visual detail that makes a French door beautiful is what makes it more vulnerable. Original wavy glass in a CWE or Lafayette Square home, set in wood that's been there a hundred years, deserves more than a rushed coat with a brush. Done correctly, a refinished French door will last as long as any solid slab door. Done incorrectly, the finish fails at the glass edges within a season.

### H2: Hand prep — no shortcuts, no dip tanks.

French doors cannot go through a chemical dip tank. The glass can't be submerged. But more importantly: even if the glass weren't there, dipping would be wrong. The profiles, the muntins, the meeting rails on a well-made French door have detail that dip-tank chemistry softens and obscures. The geometry comes back wrong.

We hand-strip every muntin, every profile, every glass edge — masked precisely where the glass meets the wood so that stripping doesn't fog or etch the panes. For original wavy or leaded glass, which is irreplaceable in older St. Louis homes, the masking process is careful and methodical.

The extra time is visible in the result. A French door finished this way looks like a French door, not a processed piece of wood with glass in it.

### H2: Glazing and caulk — the part most people miss.

When a French door comes in for refinishing, we assess the glazing compound around each glass panel. Glazing compound cures, shrinks, and cracks over time — especially on older doors that have never been re-glazed. Cracked glazing is both a moisture point and a finish failure point: the topcoat can't bridge over a crack in the substrate.

Where glazing compound is failing, we re-glaze before finishing. For caulk at the glass-to-wood transition on more modern doors, we assess and replace as needed. This is part of the refinishing scope, not an add-on.

### H2: Where French doors appear — and why they matter.

French doors in St. Louis homes appear most commonly in three locations: between a formal dining room and the living area, as the passage to a sunroom or rear parlor, and as patio doors in additions or renovated spaces. In each of these locations, the doors are seen from both sides and usually have natural light passing through them — which means any inconsistency in the finish is immediately visible.

We refinish French doors as a complete unit: door slab, frame, and both sides of the door, finished to read consistently from every angle. For paired French doors, both doors are done at the same time so they match.

### H2: Board-up method — shop work where possible.

Where French doors can be safely removed, we take them to the shop for controlled-environment finishing. The benefit is the same as for any board-up project: stable temperature and humidity during cure, better finish leveling, no chemical smell in the house.

Large built-in French door assemblies — full-height units with fixed transoms or sidelights that can't be transported as panels — are handled on-site. We assess each installation and determine the right approach during the estimate visit.

French doors in St. Louis homes built before 1978 carry the same lead paint considerations as any other pre-1978 wood surface. The presence of multiple small wood sections and glass-to-wood transitions doesn't change the requirement — it makes careful containment more important.

**Q: Can you refinish French doors without removing the glass?**
A: Yes — glass masking is standard procedure on every French door project. We mask each pane carefully before stripping and finishing. Removing and reinstalling glass adds risk and is rarely necessary. For original wavy or antique glass, we take additional care and do not remove the glass.

**Q: My French door has original wavy glass. Is that a problem?**
A: It's a characteristic worth preserving, not a problem. Wavy or antique glass in older St. Louis homes is often irreplaceable — the process for making it hasn't been in common production for over a century. We mask it carefully and handle the door with appropriate care throughout the refinishing process.

**Q: The glazing compound around my French door glass is cracked and pulling away. Can you fix that?**
A: Yes. Re-glazing is part of what we assess and address during a French door refinishing project. Cracked or missing glazing is a moisture entry point — refinishing over it without addressing it doesn't solve the problem. We re-glaze before finishing.

**Q: Do you refinish both sides of a French door?**
A: Yes. French doors are seen from both sides, often with light coming through the glass — any inconsistency shows immediately. We finish both sides as part of the standard scope.

**Q: How long does French door refinishing take?**
A: A single French door unit typically runs 4–6 days, longer than a standard slab door given the additional hand work at each glass edge. For paired French doors, we do them together. We give you a specific timeline with the estimate.
