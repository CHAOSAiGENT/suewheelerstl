### Introduction (~80 words)

- Most restaurant bar tops are refinished reactively: the finish fails visibly and then ownership addresses it
- The better approach: a planned maintenance cycle that catches the surface before it fails and costs more to fix
- This post covers: when to refinish, what the process looks like, and how to build a maintenance schedule that protects the investment

### H2: Signs Your Bar Top Needs Refinishing — Not Just Cleaning (~150 words)
- What wear looks like vs. what dirty looks like:
  - Clean first: a cloudy or hazy finish can sometimes be restored with a proper wood-safe cleaner; don't refinish prematurely
  - Signs that cleaning won't fix it:
    - White rings or water marks that have penetrated the finish (not surface haze)
    - Visible scratches or gouges in the finish layer — not just surface marks
    - Finish lifting at the edges or joints — finish is delaminating from the substrate
    - Bare wood exposed in wear areas (particularly around drain boards and under draft pulls)
    - Tacky or sticky surface in any area — finish has broken down chemically from cleaning products
    - Uneven sheen: some areas flat, some glossy — finish is wearing inconsistently
- Rule of thumb: if you can feel the scratch with a fingernail, the finish is compromised. If you can only see it, you may have more time.
- When in doubt, call for an assessment before committing to full refinishing — a professional can tell you how much life is left

### H2: What the Refinishing Process Looks Like for a Bar Top (~150 words)
- Commercial bar top refinishing is on-prem work — the bar doesn't leave
- What the process involves:
  1. Assessment: evaluate finish condition, wood species, substrate integrity, any structural damage to the top
  2. Surface prep and containment: mask off the bar area, protect adjacent surfaces and equipment
  3. Strip: chemical strip applied by hand, removed manually — no mechanical sanding that creates dust issues in a restaurant environment
  4. Repair: fill any gouges, cracks, or depressions with appropriate filler before finishing
  5. Sand: even surface preparation, grain open for finish adhesion
  6. Apply finish: multiple coats of commercial-grade polyurethane — more coats than residential because of the abuse level; sanding between coats
  7. Cure time: 24–48 hours per coat is required; plan for 3–5 days out of service depending on scope
- Scheduling note: bar tops are typically refinished overnight or during a closed period (Monday, early AM) — coordinating around your operation matters; we work around your schedule

### H2: The Maintenance Cycle — How Often Should You Refinish? (~150 words)
- Industry baseline for a well-maintained bar top: 3–5 years between full refinishing
- What "well-maintained" means: cleaned with appropriate products (no bleach, no ammonia), minor chips addressed promptly, no standing water at seams
- What accelerates wear:
  - Draft beer residue — highly acidic, breaks down finish at draft lines faster than anywhere else
  - High-proof spirits spilled and not wiped quickly
  - Citrus juice from garnish prep (lime, lemon) — highly acidic
  - Aggressive cleaning products used for compliance purposes (food service sanitizers) — necessary but hard on finish
- What a maintenance coat does: if the finish is worn but the wood is sound, a maintenance coat (light sand + fresh topcoat) can extend life 2–3 years without full refinishing — lower cost, shorter downtime
- The smart operator's plan: schedule an annual assessment; refinish every 3–5 years; maintenance coat in between when needed

### H2: What Happens If You Wait Too Long (~100 words)
- If finish fails completely and bare wood is exposed to moisture and daily service conditions:
  - Wood swells, warps, or cracks at seams
  - Grain rises — surface becomes rough and unhygienic (bacteria can harbor in open grain)
  - Stains from citrus, spirits, and food penetrate bare wood — may require more aggressive stripping or may be permanent
  - Structural damage to the substrate (MDF, plywood core) if moisture penetrates over time — at that point, the top may need replacement, not refinishing
- Cost implication: reactive refinishing when the surface has failed costs more than planned maintenance — and replacement is significantly more expensive than either
- Frame it as operations math: planned maintenance is the cheaper option every time
