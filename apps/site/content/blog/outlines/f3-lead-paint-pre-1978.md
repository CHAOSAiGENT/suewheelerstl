### Intro (75 words)

---

### H2: The Baseline Fact About Pre-1978 Homes (150 words)
- Lead was common in residential paint until the federal ban in 1978
- In St. Louis, with its large inventory of pre-1940 housing, lead-painted woodwork is the norm rather than the exception — especially on original doors, trim, window sills, and baseboards
- The presence of lead paint doesn't mean the wood is unusable or should be replaced — it means the refinishing process has to follow specific safety protocols
- Who is at elevated risk: children under 6, pregnant women, and anyone in the home during work that disturbs lead paint
- The two primary ways lead exposure occurs in refinishing: sanding (creates airborne lead dust) and chemical stripping (creates lead-containing slurry that must be properly contained and disposed of)

---

### H2: The EPA Rule — What's Actually Required (200 words)
- The **Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule (RRP Rule)**, administered by the EPA, requires:
  - Work in pre-1978 homes (with children under 6 or pregnant women present, or with target housing that is a rental) must be performed by an **EPA Certified Lead Renovator**
  - Certified firms must follow specific work practices: containment, HEPA vacuuming, wet methods to control dust, proper disposal
  - Documentation: the certified renovator must provide notification to the homeowner before work begins and retain records of compliance
- What it does NOT require: removing lead paint from every surface in the home. The trigger is renovation, repair, or painting activity that disturbs lead paint — refinishing falls clearly within this.
- Owner-occupied primary residences where no children under 6 or pregnant women live: the RRP Rule technically has a narrower applicability, but responsible practice applies the same protocols regardless. A certified renovator won't cut corners because the rule technically allows it.
- State of Missouri: follows EPA rules; DHSS enforces in certain contexts

---

### H2: What EPA Certification Actually Means on the Job (150 words)
- EPA RRP Certification requires specific training in lead-safe work practices — not just a background check
- The certified renovator is responsible for ensuring their crew follows proper protocols, even if the crew themselves are not individually certified
- On a refinishing job: containment sheeting around the work area, HEPA-equipped tools for sanding, proper wet chemical stripping technique, containment and disposal of lead-bearing material
- What it protects: the household members during and after the work, and the contractor from liability
- What to ask any refinisher before hiring: "Are you EPA Certified?" — and ask to see the certificate number. It's verifiable.

---

### H2: Why Hiring an Uncertified Refinisher Is a Real Risk (150 words)
- The risk isn't just regulatory — it's health and liability
- An uncertified refinisher who sands lead paint without containment creates airborne lead dust that settles throughout the home
- Post-project cleanup doesn't eliminate the risk if the work practices weren't controlled during the job
- If children live in or visit the home: the health stakes are significant. Lead exposure in children causes developmental and neurological damage — there is no safe level.
- From a property standpoint: if a lead-dust contamination event is later discovered (e.g., during a sale inspection), the liability chain traces back to the renovation work
- The cost savings of hiring an uncertified refinisher are not worth the exposure
