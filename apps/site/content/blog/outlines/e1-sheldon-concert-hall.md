### Intro (100 words)

---

### H2: What Makes The Sheldon Special (150 words)
- Built 1912; Beaux-Arts architecture; one of the finest small concert halls in the country
- Original woodwork is part of what makes the acoustic and visual character of the space
- $11 million renovation project — part of an ongoing stewardship commitment by The Sheldon's leadership
- The doors: original to the building, significant in scale and detail; they're what guests see and touch every time they enter
- Why it matters to get it right: in a landmark building, the woodwork is not decorative. It is part of the architectural record of the city.

---

### H2: The Scope — What Was Done and Why (200 words)
- Door refinishing: existing finish assessed, hand-stripped back to bare wood, condition evaluated, repairs made, new finish applied
- The process on a landmark building cannot shortcut anything — every step documented
- Color matching: the finished doors needed to match the original historic appearance, which requires careful stain selection and test panels
- Finish selection: appropriate sheen level for a historic venue — not a high-gloss modern look
- On-premises work in an active building: scheduling around events, protecting surrounding surfaces, managing fume exposure in a shared public space
- EPA Certified Lead Renovator certification: critical for a building of this age — proper lead-safe work practices required throughout

---

### H2: What Institutional Work Requires That Residential Doesn't (200 words)
- Scale: a single institutional door can be twice the size of a residential door
- Documentation: institutional clients need work documented — scope, method, products used, chain of custody
- Scheduling constraints: concert halls, churches, universities don't close for a week. Work happens in off-hours, between events, in stages.
- Compliance: institutional work often has preservation standards built into the contract — Secretary of Interior Standards or local landmark designation requirements
- Coordination: working alongside other trades on a renovation project requires sequencing discipline — the wood needs to be done before certain other work, and protected after
- Liability awareness: in a public building with high foot traffic, a finish that off-gasses too long or creates a slip hazard is not acceptable

---

### H2: What This Project Means for Other Institutional Clients (150 words)
- The Sheldon is a proof point, not an anomaly
- Churches, universities, libraries, concert venues across St. Louis have original woodwork that is aging the same way
- The mistake many institutions make: treating maintenance and refinishing as discretionary, and then facing emergency replacement costs when the wood is genuinely damaged
- Sue's institutional work spans churches, university buildings, and public spaces — not just The Sheldon
- The credential matters: when a client's board or facilities committee needs to approve a contractor, a name like The Sheldon on the reference list changes the conversation

---

### H2: Starting an Institutional Refinishing Project (100 words)
- The inquiry process for institutional work differs from residential: longer lead time, often multiple decision-makers, sometimes RFP or proposal process
- What to bring to the first conversation: a description of the scope (what surfaces, how many doors/how much millwork, what condition), any preservation or compliance requirements, and your scheduling constraints
- Sue handles institutional inquiries directly — she's the one who has to look at the wood before scoping the work
