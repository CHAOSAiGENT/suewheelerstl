## Outline

### Introduction (~100 words)
- Open with the question homeowners don't know to ask: "How do you remove the old finish?"
- Two answers exist in this industry: dip it in a chemical tank, or strip it by hand
- One answer is faster and cheaper for the refinisher. The other is better for your wood.
- Frame the post: this is what every homeowner should know before hiring anyone to touch their wood.
- Internal link: [About Sue Wheeler](/about/) — "We've hand-stripped every piece for 36 years"

### H2: What Is Dip-Stripping? (~150 words)
- Explain the process: pieces are submerged in a tank of caustic chemicals (lye-based or acid-based) to dissolve old finish
- Common in furniture refinishing shops and lower-end refinishers
- Fast and cheap — one person can process many pieces at once
- Why shops default to it: it requires less skilled labor, less time per piece
- Bullet: What happens in the tank:
  - Chemical raises the wood grain (permanently roughens surface texture)
  - Hot-tank dipping can destroy or loosen glue joints — especially on panel doors and frames
  - Can warp thin wood panels from prolonged liquid exposure
  - Strips color from natural wood (stain base becomes unpredictable after dipping)
  - Removes patina from antique or historic pieces — irreversible
- Note: Some shops offer "cold tank" dipping as a softer alternative — same grain-raising problems, fewer glue joint failures, still not recommended for fine architectural wood

### H2: What Is Hand-Stripping? (~150 words)
- Define the process: chemical stripper applied by hand, allowed to work, then removed manually with scrapers, steel wool, and detail tools
- Slower, more labor-intensive, requires skilled hands — each piece is worked individually
- Why it matters: the refinisher controls what's removed and what's preserved
- Bullet: What hand-stripping preserves:
  - Wood grain texture stays intact — finish bonds properly to a smooth surface
  - Glue joints remain undisturbed — doors, frames, and panels stay tight
  - Natural color of the wood is preserved — stain penetrates predictably
  - Patina and character of antique and historic pieces remains
  - Detail and profile of carved or shaped wood is not eroded by chemical soaking
- Sue's process: chemical stripper applied, worked by hand with scrapers and brushes, followed by neutralizing wash and light sanding before any stain or finish is applied


### H2: Side-by-Side Comparison (~150 words)
- Use a simple comparison table or parallel bullets (developer can render as table)
- Dimensions to compare:
  - Speed: Dip = fast / Hand = time-intensive
  - Wood grain: Dip = raised and roughened / Hand = preserved
  - Glue joints: Dip = at risk / Hand = undisturbed
  - Stain predictability: Dip = unpredictable / Hand = consistent
  - Historic/antique suitability: Dip = no / Hand = yes
  - NPS Standards compliance: Dip = likely disqualifying / Hand = compliant
  - Cost to refinisher: Dip = lower / Hand = higher
  - Cost to you in the long run: Dip = higher (poor results, sooner re-do) / Hand = lower
- Closing note on this section: "The method your refinisher uses isn't a minor detail — it's the difference between a result that lasts and one that fails."

### H2: Why It Matters Especially for Historic and Pre-1978 Homes (~150 words)
- National Park Service (Secretary of Interior's Standards) recommends hand-stripping for historic wood — dipping can disqualify a project from tax credit compliance
- Historic wood elements — pocket doors, original millwork, built-in hutches — often have glue joints that won't survive a tank
- Lead paint consideration: dipping doesn't safely contain lead-contaminated stripping waste; hand-strip with lead-safe protocol does

### H2: Questions to Ask Any Wood Refinisher (~100 words)
- Before you hire, ask:
  - Do you hand-strip or dip?
  - If you dip, do you offer hand-strip as an option?
  - How do you handle lead paint on pre-1978 pieces?
  - Are you EPA Certified for lead renovation?
- Note: A refinisher who dips may not tell you upfront — ask explicitly
- Frame: "The answers tell you a lot about what you're actually buying."
