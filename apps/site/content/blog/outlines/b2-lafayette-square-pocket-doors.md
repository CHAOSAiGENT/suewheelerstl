## Meta Description
Lafayette Square Victorian homes are famous for their pocket doors — and most of them haven't been touched in decades. Here's what makes them special, what restoring them involves, and what to watch out for.

## Outline

### Introduction (~80 words)
- Lafayette Square is one of St. Louis's oldest intact Victorian neighborhoods — 1870s–1900s homes built at a time when pocket doors were standard in formal rooms
- Most of these doors are still in the walls — but they may be painted shut, warped, or so layered with old finish they no longer slide properly
- The good news: most can be restored without replacement
- The specific challenge: pocket doors are surface-only access items — the door is in the wall; you can't take it out without major construction
- Internal link: [Neighborhoods — Lafayette Square](/neighborhoods/lafayette-square/)

### H2: What Makes Lafayette Square Pocket Doors Worth Saving (~130 words)
- Age and material: most Lafayette Square pocket doors are late 19th-century, made from old-growth oak, chestnut, or walnut — dense, tight-grained wood that no longer exists in production lumber
- Scale and proportion: original pocket doors are often full ceiling height — 9, 10, or 11 feet — sized to the rooms they serve; replacement doors of equivalent size are custom-made and expensive
- Hardware: original mortise hardware (pulls, edge hardware) is period-specific and often irreplaceable without significant cost; refinishing preserves it
- Architectural coherence: these doors match the original millwork program of the house — the profiles, the panel design, the species — replacement interrupts that continuity
- The character argument: a restored original pocket door looks and feels different from anything built today; the wood has age and density you can't replicate

### H2: The Challenge of Refinishing a Pocket Door in Place (~150 words)
- Why pocket doors are harder than regular doors:
  - They're in the wall — you can't remove them for shop work without major construction (opening the wall, removing the pocket frame)
  - Access is surface-only: the refinisher works on the visible face of the door as it slides partially out from each side
  - Most have been painted over multiple times — accumulated coats obscure panel detail and hardware
  - Old paint on pre-1978 homes almost certainly contains lead — EPA-certified lead-safe protocol is required
- What proper in-place refinishing involves:
  - EPA containment setup before any stripping begins
  - Hand-strip applied in sections as door is slid to accessible positions
  - Detail work on panel profiles, molding edges, and hardware surrounds
  - Stain application and multiple polyurethane topcoats — in place, section by section
  - Full hardware masking and cleanup
- What it cannot do: address structural issues with the pocket mechanism (rollers, track) — those are carpentry, not refinishing; Sue can identify them and refer out

### H2: Lead Paint and Lafayette Square (~100 words)
- Any Lafayette Square home built before 1978 (essentially all of them) must be treated as a potential lead paint site
- Original painted surfaces on pocket doors — especially those that have been repainted multiple times — almost certainly contain lead
- EPA RRP Rule requirements: contractor must be EPA Certified; containment required; lead-safe stripping protocol; proper disposal; post-work documentation
- Sue Wheeler is EPA Certified — this is not optional or an upgrade; it's standard for every pre-1978 project
- What this means for you as a homeowner: your family is protected during the work; you receive documentation of lead-safe completion; you're in compliance with federal law

### H2: Historic Tax Credits and Lafayette Square Pocket Doors (~100 words)
- Lafayette Square is a St. Louis Certified Local Historic District — qualifying projects may be eligible for Missouri's state historic tax credit (up to 35%) and federal historic tax credit (20%)
- Refinishing original architectural woodwork like pocket doors can be a qualifying expenditure in a certified rehabilitation project
- What this means: your refinishing costs may be partially offset by state and federal credits if you are doing a broader certified rehabilitation
- Note: tax credit qualification requires project pre-approval from MO SHPO — encourage readers to start that conversation early
