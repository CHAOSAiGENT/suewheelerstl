### Intro (75 words)

---

### H2: What the Secretary of the Interior's Standards Are (150 words)
- Published by the National Park Service; used by SHPO for tax credit certification and federal grant compliance
- Four treatment approaches: Preservation, Rehabilitation, Restoration, Reconstruction — most residential and commercial work falls under Rehabilitation
- The Standards aren't law by default, but they become binding when: (a) federal or state historic tax credits are involved, (b) federal grants or assistance are involved, (c) local historic district regulations adopt them by reference
- Why homeowners and architects should know them regardless: they represent best practice for maintaining historic material integrity, even without a tax credit in play

---

### H2: What the Standards Say About Wood Specifically (200 words)
- Standard 2: Preserve the historic character — don't alter features that define the property's significance
- Standard 6: Deteriorated features shall be repaired rather than replaced (the key one for wood)
- Standard 9: New additions or alterations shall not destroy historic materials — applies when existing wood is adjacent to new construction
- Practical translation for wood refinishing: the goal is to restore, not replace. If original wood can be repaired (including refinished), it should be. Replacing it with new wood — even identical-profile new wood — is a Standards violation in most cases.
- Also relevant: the treatment must not damage or destroy historic material. This is where method matters enormously.

---

### H2: Three Methods Compared Against the Standards (250 words)

#### Hand-Strip Chemical Refinishing
- Controlled, surface-only process — removes existing finish without affecting the wood fiber beneath
- Preserves original grain patterns, tool marks, and surface character
- No mechanical abrasion beyond light sanding for adhesion
- Aligns cleanly with Standards 2 and 6 — this is the recommended approach

#### Chemical Dip-Stripping
- Submersion process: removes all finish (and often original surface patina)
- Can raise grain significantly, loosen mortise-and-tenon joints, cause checking and warping in older wood
- Removes the surface character that makes historic wood legible
- SHPO reviewers and preservation architects increasingly flag dip-stripping as non-compliant — not by rule but by practical effect
- If you're pursuing tax credits, using a dip-stripper is a risk

#### Mechanical Sanding / Sandblasting
- Aggressive mechanical abrasion removes wood fiber, not just finish
- Can eliminate original surface profiles — particularly on carved or routed details
- Sandblasting on millwork is widely considered destructive to historic fabric
- These methods will typically fail Standards review

### H2: What This Means If You're Pursuing Tax Credits (150 words)
- SHPO review looks at method as part of the work plan — your refinisher's process is part of the documentation
- Documenting hand-strip method: what chemicals, what neutralization, what final finish
- Finish specification: for historic projects, the finish should not obscure original wood character — oil-based polyurethane in appropriate sheen levels is generally acceptable; film-forming finishes that dramatically alter appearance (high-gloss on a matte original) may raise questions
- Practical advice: get your SHPO liaison involved before the work begins, not after

### H2: Even Without Tax Credits, the Standards Are a Useful Guide (150 words)
- For homeowners who just want to do right by their historic home, the Standards are the clearest published framework available
- They represent accumulated professional judgment from preservation architects, historians, and conservators
- Following them — even informally — protects the material integrity and long-term value of original woodwork
- They're also increasingly cited in local historic district review, even when no federal program is involved
- Short version: if your refinisher doesn't know what the Standards are, ask why
