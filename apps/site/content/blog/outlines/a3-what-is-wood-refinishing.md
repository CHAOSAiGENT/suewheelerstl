### H2: The Basic Definition (~100 words)
- Wood refinishing = the process of removing an existing finish (paint, stain, polyurethane, lacquer) down to bare wood, then applying a new stain and finish
- "Finish" refers to the protective coating — not just color; it's what protects the wood from wear, moisture, and UV
- Two core steps:
  - Strip: remove the old finish (chemically, mechanically, or both)
  - Refinish: apply new stain and protective topcoat (polyurethane in most architectural applications)
- Note: stripping method matters significantly — see [Hand-Strip vs. Dip](/blog/hand-strip-vs-dip-wood-refinishing/) for why

### H2: Refinishing vs. Repainting — What's the Difference? (~150 words)
- The most common confusion: homeowners assume painting and refinishing are interchangeable
- Repainting = applying a new coat of paint on top of existing paint or finish. The old finish stays. You're adding a layer.
- Refinishing = removing everything down to bare wood, then starting fresh. Nothing from the old finish remains.
- Why it matters:
  - Painted-over wood hides the grain — refinishing restores and showcases it
  - Multiple layers of paint can obscure detail in carved or profiled millwork
  - Paint traps moisture and leads to peeling; proper refinishing with polyurethane creates a durable, flexible bond
  - If original stained wood has been painted over, refinishing can recover the natural wood underneath
- Bullet: When to repaint vs. refinish:
  - Repaint: cabinet boxes that were always painted, walls, trim that will be painted
  - Refinish: stained wood that has dulled, peeled, or needs restoration; painted wood over natural grain you want to recover

### H2: What Does the Process Actually Look Like? (~150 words)
- Walk through the standard steps:
  1. Assessment — Sue evaluates the existing finish, wood species, condition, and any damage or repairs needed
  2. Strip — chemical stripper applied by hand (never dipped); old finish removed down to bare wood
  3. Repair — any gouges, checks, or damage addressed before finish is applied (see service pages for repair scope)
  4. Sand — wood sanded to even out surface and open grain for stain penetration
  5. Stain — color applied (or natural/clear if no color change desired); wiped and allowed to penetrate
  6. Finish — polyurethane applied in multiple coats; each coat sanded between applications
  7. Final inspection — piece checked and approved before delivery or on-prem completion
- Note on board-up vs. on-prem: most pieces are transported to Sue's shop for better quality control — doors, frames, and smaller millwork items leave the home and return refinished

### H2: What Can Refinishing Fix? (~120 words)
- Refinishing can address:
  - Faded or yellowed finish (UV damage)
  - Peeling or flaking topcoat
  - Worn areas on high-traffic surfaces (stair treads, cabinet doors)
  - Water stains and minor discoloration
  - Color that's no longer wanted (stain change)
  - Surface scratches that haven't penetrated to bare wood
  - Painted-over wood that has original grain underneath
- What refinishing cannot fix alone:
  - Structural damage (broken joints, split panels) — repair work precedes refinishing
  - Deep gouges below the surface — epoxy fill or patch required first
  - Wood that has rotted or is no longer structurally sound — replacement needed
- Note: Sue's team handles repair-before-finish as part of the standard process — this is not a separate engagement; it's part of an honest assessment

### H2: What Types of Wood Can Be Refinished? (~100 words)
- Architectural woodwork refinishing (Sue's specialty):
  - Kitchen cabinets (doors, drawer fronts, frames)
  - Interior and exterior doors
  - Staircases (treads, risers, spindles, newel posts, railings)
  - Built-ins (hutches, bookcases, buffets)
  - Room millwork (baseboards, crown molding, wainscoting, chair rail, mantels, window trim)
  - Commercial wood (bar tops, tabletops, booth wood, hotel millwork)
  - Institutional wood (concert hall doors, church pews, university casework)
- What is NOT refinished:
  - Freestanding furniture — Sue focuses on architectural and built-in wood only
  - New wood that has never been finished (different process — consult separately)
